    Percent error vs percent difference vs uncertainty

    These sound alike but answer different questions. Choose by whether one of your values is a trusted reference.

    compare_arrows

    Percent error

    Use when you compare a measurement with a known accepted value. The accepted value is the denominator, so the answer says how far off you were relative to the truth.

    |measured − accepted| ÷ |accepted| × 100
    functions

    Percent difference

    Use when comparing two measurements and neither is a reference. The average of the two values is the denominator, so the order does not matter.

    |A − B| ÷ ((A + B) ÷ 2) × 100
    ssid_chart

    Error vs uncertainty

    Error is the difference from the true value, which is normally unknown. Uncertainty describes the doubt about a measurement. A percent error smaller than your uncertainty is consistent with the accepted value.

    Worked examples

    Illustrative measurements, each checked by hand. Use the presets above to reproduce the first, third and fourth.

    Pendulum measurement of gravity

    A student measures g = 9.62 m/s²; the standard value is 9.80665 m/s².

    |9.62 − 9.80665| = 0.18665
    0.18665 ÷ 9.80665 = 0.019033
    percent error = 1.9033% (under)

    Titration molarity

    A solution prepared as 0.1000 M measures 0.1008 M.

    |0.1008 − 0.1000| = 0.0008
    0.0008 ÷ 0.1000 = 0.008
    percent error = 0.8000% (over)

    Machined shaft

    A 50.000 mm shaft measures 50.042 mm against a ±0.050 mm tolerance.

    |50.042 − 50.000| = 0.042 mm
    0.042 ÷ 50 = 0.00084
    percent error = 0.084%
    0.042 ≤ 0.050 → within tolerance

    What is percent error?

    Percent error expresses how far a measured (experimental) value is from an accepted (theoretical) value, as a percentage of the accepted value. A small percent error means the measurement is close to the reference. It is common in physics, chemistry and engineering labs.

    How to calculate percent error

    • Subtract: measured − accepted. This is the signed error.
    • Take the absolute value to get the absolute error, |Δ|.
    • Divide by the absolute value of the accepted value for the relative error.
    • Multiply by 100 to get percent error.

    Some courses keep the sign (negative = under-measured, positive = over-measured) and others use the absolute value; this page shows both.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can percent error be negative?expand_more

    By the strict textbook definition, percent error is the absolute value and so never negative. Many labs keep the sign to show direction: negative means your measurement is below the accepted value, positive means above. Switch the Sign setting to choose.

    What is an acceptable percent error?expand_more

    It depends on the field and the purpose. A teaching lab may accept a few percent, while precision manufacturing or analytical chemistry may require well under 1%. Use the tolerance check with the limit your course, standard or specification sets.

    What if the theoretical value is zero?expand_more

    Percent error is undefined because it divides by the accepted value. Report the absolute error with its units instead, or use an absolute tolerance in the tolerance check.

    How do significant figures apply?expand_more

    Round only the final answer, not the intermediate steps. The significant-figures setting rounds the displayed results; the calculation itself always uses full precision.

    What is the difference between percent error and percent difference?expand_more

    Percent error compares a measurement with an accepted reference value and divides by that value. Percent difference compares two measurements when neither is a reference and divides by their average.

    Why is percent error not the same as uncertainty?expand_more

    Percent error needs a known accepted value, whereas uncertainty describes the doubt in the measurement itself. A percent error smaller than the measurement uncertainty means the result agrees with the accepted value within what the measurement can resolve.

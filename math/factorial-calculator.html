    What is a factorial?

    The factorial n! is the product of all whole numbers from 1 to n, with 0! = 1 by convention (there is exactly one way to arrange nothing). It counts the ways to order n distinct objects and grows faster than any exponential: 10! = 3,628,800 and 20! ≈ 2.43 × 10¹⁸.

    Useful facts

    • Trailing zeros (Legendre): the number of factors of a prime p in n! is Σ ⌊n/pᵏ⌋. Zeros equal the number of factors of 5.
    • Stirling’s approximation: n! ≈ √(2πn)(n/e)ⁿ, with relative error about 1/(12n).
    • Ramanujan’s refinement: n! ≈ √π (n/e)ⁿ (8n³ + 4n² + n + 1/30)^(1/6), far more accurate.
    • Gamma function: Γ(n + 1) = n!, extending factorials to real and complex numbers.

    Frequently asked questions

    Why is 0! equal to 1?expand_more

    Because there is exactly one way to arrange zero objects (do nothing), and because it keeps the recurrence n! = n · (n − 1)! true at n = 1 (1 = 1 · 0!). It also makes formulas such as C(n, 0) = n! ÷ (0! n!) = 1 work.

    How does Legendre’s formula count trailing zeros without multiplying everything?expand_more

    Each trailing zero needs one factor 10 = 2 × 5. Factors of 2 are more plentiful than 5s, so zeros equal the number of 5s in n!. Multiples of 5 contribute one each, multiples of 25 one more, multiples of 125 one more, and so on: Σ ⌊n/5ᵏ⌋.

    What is the difference between Stirling’s formula and Ramanujan’s correction?expand_more

    Stirling’s leading term underestimates n! by roughly 1/(12n) in relative terms (0.83% at n = 10). Ramanujan’s version adds a cubic term under a sixth root, shrinking the relative error to about 9 × 10⁻⁸ at n = 10 (under 0.00001%).

    How does this calculator avoid overflow?expand_more

    Ordinary 64-bit floating point overflows at 171! (about 10³⁰⁸). This page uses arbitrary-size integers for exact results up to 3,000!, and logarithms for the Stirling comparisons, so nothing overflows.

    Is the double factorial (n!!) the factorial of a factorial?expand_more

    No. n!! multiplies every second number down from n (10!! = 10 · 8 · 6 · 4 · 2 = 3,840). The factorial of a factorial would be (n!)!, which is astronomically larger.

    Can I take the factorial of a negative number or a fraction?expand_more

    Not with the whole-number definition. The Gamma function extends it to all real numbers except the negative integers, where it has poles: (−1)!, (−2)!, … are undefined. Use the Gamma tab for fractional values.

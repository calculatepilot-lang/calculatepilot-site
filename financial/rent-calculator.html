The 30% rule

Spending no more than 30% of gross income on rent is a commonly cited affordability guideline, though it's a general rule of thumb — your comfortable number depends on other debts, savings goals, and cost of living.

Beyond the 30% rule

The 30% guideline is a useful starting point, but your actual comfortable rent budget depends on other factors: existing debt payments, savings goals, commute costs, and local cost of living all matter. Someone with no debt and low other expenses might comfortably afford more than 30%, while someone with significant student loans or car payments may want to budget less.

It's also worth factoring in renter's insurance, utilities, and moving costs when budgeting for a new place — the advertised rent is rarely the complete monthly housing cost.

A worked example

At $6,200/month gross income with a 30% target, the recommended maximum rent is $1,860/month, or about $22,320 annually.

If that same person instead budgeted 25% (a more conservative target, perhaps due to other debt), the maximum rent drops to $1,550/month — a $310 difference that could instead go toward savings or debt payoff.

Other costs of renting to budget for

Beyond the base rent figure, renters typically budget for utilities, renters insurance, parking, and sometimes pet fees — all of which add to the effective monthly housing cost beyond what the 30% guideline alone captures.

In competitive rental markets, landlords may also require proof of income at a multiple of monthly rent (commonly 2.5-3x rent) as a qualifying condition, which is a related but separate calculation from the affordability guideline this calculator uses.

Frequently asked questions

Is 30% the right target for everyone?

It's a widely used guideline, but people with significant other debts may want to budget less, while those with minimal other expenses may comfortably afford more.

What if 30% of my income isn't enough rent for my area?

In high cost-of-living areas, many renters exceed the 30% guideline out of necessity — if so, it's worth budgeting extra carefully elsewhere and building a larger emergency fund to compensate.

Should I include roommates' contributions in this calculation?

No — calculate based on your own income and your own share of the rent, to get an accurate picture of what you personally can afford.

Do landlords typically require a minimum income relative to rent?

Many landlords require monthly income of roughly 2.5-3x the rent amount as a qualifying condition, which is a common industry practice separate from personal affordability guidelines.

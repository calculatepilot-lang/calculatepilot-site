functionsAPY vs APR: The Compounding Spread

The APR is the stated annual rate before compounding. The APY is what you actually earn in a year once interest compounds:

APY = (1 + r / n)n − 1r = stated APR, n = compounding periods per year

    Why CDs offer predictable, fixed returns

    Unlike a savings account, a CD locks in both your rate and your money for a fixed term — in exchange, banks typically offer a higher rate than a standard savings account, since they can rely on the deposit staying put for the full term.

    The trade-off is liquidity: withdrawing funds before the CD matures usually triggers an early withdrawal penalty, often equal to several months of interest, so CDs work best for money you're confident you won't need during the term.

    Comparing CD terms and rates

    Longer-term CDs don't always offer meaningfully higher rates than shorter-term ones — rate curves shift with the broader interest rate environment, so it's worth comparing actual offered rates across terms rather than assuming longer always means better.

    A common strategy called 'CD laddering' spreads deposits across multiple CDs with staggered maturity dates, balancing the higher rates of longer terms with periodic access to some of your funds.

    How CD interest is calculated

    A CD pays a fixed rate for a fixed term, so the maturity value follows one formula. The calculator above uses the APY, which already includes compounding:

    Maturity value = deposit × (1 + APY)years, and interest earned = maturity value − deposit.

    TermMaturity valueInterest earned
    6 months$10,222.52$222.52
    12 months$10,450.00$450.00
    18 months$10,682.54$682.54
    24 months$10,920.25$920.25
    60 months$12,461.82$2,461.82

    The table shows a $10,000 deposit at 4.50% APY. Use it as a quick check on the calculator's result. If a bank quotes only an interest rate, convert it first with APY = (1 + r ÷ n)n − 1.

    Does compounding frequency matter?

    For a stated interest rate it does, a little. The more often interest compounds, the higher the APY. Here is $10,000 at a 5.00% stated rate held for one year:

    CompoundingBalance after 1 yearAPY
    Annually$10,500.005.0000%
    Semi-annually$10,506.255.0625%
    Quarterly$10,509.455.0945%
    Monthly$10,511.625.1162%
    Daily$10,512.675.1267%

    The gap between annual and daily compounding is only $12.67 on $10,000. When you compare CDs, compare APYs rather than interest rates, so compounding is already counted.

    Taxes on CD interest

    CD interest is taxed as ordinary income, usually in the year it is credited to you. Your bank reports it on Form 1099-INT, normally when you earn $10 or more in a year. For a CD longer than one year you generally report the interest each year, even if it is paid at maturity. CDs held inside an IRA are not taxed until you withdraw. Here is the effect on $10,000 at 4.50% APY for 12 months ($450 of interest):

    Early withdrawal penalty examples

    Banks usually charge a set number of days of interest, and the number often grows with the term. Always check your own disclosure, because the rules vary. On $10,000 at 4.50%, a penalty measured in simple interest looks like this:

    PenaltyApproximate cost
    90 days of interest$110.96
    180 days of interest$221.92
    365 days of interest$450.00

    Break the same CD after six months and you have earned about $222.52. A 90-day penalty takes back about $110.96, leaving roughly $111.56 of interest. If you have earned less than the penalty, the bank can take the difference from your principal.

    Is your CD insured?

    CDs at FDIC-insured banks are covered up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. Credit union CDs have the same limit through the NCUA. A CD that is inside the limit keeps its principal and accrued interest safe if the institution fails. Check that a brokered CD is issued by an insured bank.

    What happens when a CD matures

    Most banks give a short grace period after maturity, often around 7 to 10 days, to withdraw the money or change the term. If you do nothing, many CDs renew automatically for a similar term at the rate on offer that day, which can be lower than the one you had.     Real return after inflation

    A CD's rate is fixed, but prices are not. Real return = (1 + APY) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1. At 4.50% APY and 3.00% inflation, the real return is 1.045 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = 1.46%. If inflation is higher than your APY, your money buys less at maturity even though the balance grew.

    Common mistakes when using a CD calculator

    • Entering an interest rate in an APY box. They are not the same figure, so check which one your bank quotes.
    • Forgetting that interest is taxable, so the amount you keep is lower than the amount shown.
    • Ignoring the early withdrawal penalty on money you might need.
    • Letting a CD renew without comparing the new rate with other CDs and savings accounts.
    • Putting money you may need for emergencies in a CD. Build your emergency fund first.

    Frequently asked questions

    What happens if I withdraw from a CD early?expand_more

    Most CDs charge an early withdrawal penalty, commonly equal to a few months of interest, which can reduce or even eliminate the interest earned depending on how early you withdraw.

    Is a CD better than a savings account?expand_more

    CDs typically offer higher fixed rates in exchange for locking up your money for a set term, while savings accounts offer more flexibility with generally lower, variable rates — the better choice depends on whether you need access to the funds.

    How do I calculate CD interest?expand_more

    Multiply the deposit by (1 + APY) raised to the number of years, then subtract the deposit. A $10,000 CD at 4.50% APY for 12 months earns $450, and for 24 months it earns $920.25.

    What is the difference between APY and the interest rate on a CD?expand_more

    The interest rate is the stated annual rate before compounding. APY is the amount you actually earn in a year once compounding is included, so it is the better figure for comparing CDs. A 5.00% rate compounded daily is a 5.1267% APY.

    Does compounding frequency matter for a CD?expand_more

    A little. On $10,000 at a 5.00% stated rate for one year, annual compounding gives $10,500.00 and daily compounding gives $10,512.67. If you compare CDs by APY, compounding is already counted.

    Is CD interest taxable?expand_more

    Yes. CD interest is taxed as ordinary income in the year it is credited, and your bank reports it on Form 1099-INT. In a 24% bracket, $450 of interest leaves about $342 after federal tax. CDs inside an IRA are not taxed until withdrawal.

    Can you lose money in a CD?expand_more

    Not from market moves, and insured CDs are protected up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. You can still lose value if an early withdrawal penalty is larger than the interest you have earned, or if inflation is higher than your fixed rate.

    How much does a $10,000 CD earn in one year?expand_more

    At 4.00% APY it earns $400, at 4.50% it earns $450 and at 5.00% it earns $500, before tax. Enter your own rate and term in the calculator for an exact figure.

    What happens when a CD matures?expand_more

    Most banks give a grace period, often about 7 to 10 days, to withdraw the money or change the term. If you do nothing, many CDs renew automatically at the current rate, which may be lower than your old rate.

    What is a no-penalty CD?expand_more

    A no-penalty CD lets you withdraw your money after an initial waiting period without paying an early withdrawal penalty. In return, its rate is usually lower than a traditional CD of the same term.

    What is a CD ladder?expand_more

    A CD ladder splits your money across several CDs with different maturity dates, such as one to five years. One CD matures each year, so you get regular access to cash while the rest keeps earning longer-term rates.

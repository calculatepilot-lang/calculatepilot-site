How amortization works

Every fixed-rate loan payment is split between interest and principal, but that split is not constant. Interest is charged on whatever you still owe, so at the start of a loan — when the balance is at its largest — most of your payment covers interest and only a small slice reduces the debt. As the balance falls, the interest portion falls with it and the principal portion grows. The payment amount never changes; only the split does.

Take a $250,000 mortgage at 6.5% over 30 years. The monthly payment is $1,580.17. In the very first payment, $1,354.17 goes to interest and just $226.00 reduces the balance. In the final payment, the position is almost exactly reversed: $8.51 interest, $1,571.66 principal.

That asymmetry is the single most important thing to understand about long-term borrowing, and it is why the total cost of a loan bears so little resemblance to the amount you borrowed. Over 30 years, that $250,000 mortgage costs $568,861 in total — $318,861 of it interest. You repay more than twice what you borrowed.

When does the crossover happen?

The crossover point is the payment at which principal finally exceeds interest. It is later than most people expect, and considerably later than the halfway point of the term.

On that $250,000 mortgage at 6.5%, the crossover falls at payment 233 — year 19.4 of a 30-year term. For nearly two decades, more of every payment goes to the lender than to your own equity.

The equity picture is starker still:

  • After 5 years you have repaid 6.4% of the principal. The balance is $234,027.
  • After 10 years: 15.2% repaid, balance $211,940.
  • After 15 years — the halfway mark — only 27.4% repaid, balance $181,398.
  • After 20 years: 44.3% repaid, balance $139,163.

You do not repay half the principal until payment 257, in year 21.4. Two-thirds of the way through the term, you still owe more than half the original loan.

Shorter loans behave completely differently. On a $30,000 auto loan at 7% over 5 years, the payment is $594.04 and principal exceeds interest from the very first payment — $175.00 interest against $419.04 principal. Total interest over the full term is $5,642, around 19% of the amount borrowed. The shorter the term and the smaller the balance relative to the payment, the less the amortization curve distorts things.

What the term length actually costs you

Lengthening the term lowers the monthly payment. It does not make the loan cheaper — it makes it considerably more expensive, because you are borrowing the same money for twice as long.

The same $250,000 at 6.5%:

  • 30-year term: $1,580.17 per month, $318,861 total interest.
  • 15-year term: $2,177.77 per month, $141,998 total interest.

The 15-year loan costs $598 more each month. In exchange it saves $176,863 in interest — more than 70% of the original loan amount. Whether that trade is worth making depends entirely on whether the higher payment is comfortable, because a payment you cannot sustain is a worse outcome than any amount of interest.

This is the calculation lenders rarely put in front of you. The affordability conversation is framed around the monthly payment, which naturally steers borrowers toward longer terms. The total-cost conversation points the other way.

The effect of the interest rate

Small differences in rate compound into large differences in cost over a long term. On the same $250,000 over 30 years:

  • 5.5% — $1,419.47 per month, $261,010 total interest
  • 6.0% — $1,498.88 per month, $289,595 total interest
  • 6.5% — $1,580.17 per month, $318,861 total interest
  • 7.0% — $1,663.26 per month, $348,772 total interest
  • 7.5% — $1,748.04 per month, $379,293 total interest

A single percentage point — 6.5% to 7.5% — adds $167.87 to the monthly payment and $60,432 to the lifetime cost. Half a point is worth roughly $30,000. That is why shopping several lenders is worth real effort on a mortgage in a way it simply is not on a small personal loan.

Why extra payments work so well early

Every extra dollar you pay goes entirely to principal. It does not just reduce the balance by a dollar — it eliminates all the future interest that dollar would have accrued for the remaining term. Early in a long loan, that is a lot of interest.

On the $250,000 mortgage at 6.5%, adding $200 a month from the start:

  • Loan clears in 265 payments instead of 360 — 22.1 years, nearly 8 years early
  • Total interest falls from $318,861 to $221,243
  • Saving: $97,618

You pay in roughly $53,000 of extra principal and save almost double that in interest. The same $200 a month started in year 20 saves a small fraction of that, because by then there is little remaining term for the interest to accrue over. Timing dominates.

Two practical cautions. First, check for prepayment penalties — now uncommon on US mortgages and auto loans, but they do still exist, particularly on some private and subprime products. Second, make sure the servicer applies extra money to principal rather than treating it as an advance on next month's payment. That usually requires ticking a specific box or adding a note; if it is applied as a prepaid instalment, you get none of the benefit described above.

Amortizing loans versus other structures

Not every loan amortizes. Recognising the difference matters, because the repayment behaviour is completely different.

Amortizing loans — mortgages, auto loans, most personal and student loans — have equal payments that fully clear the debt by the end of the term. That is what this calculator models.

Interest-only loans require only the interest for an initial period. The balance does not move at all during that time, so no equity accrues. When the interest-only period ends, payments jump sharply because the full principal must now be repaid over a shorter remaining term.

Balloon loans amortize on a long schedule but end early with a single large lump sum. The monthly payment looks like a 30-year mortgage; the balloon at year 5 or 7 does not.

Revolving credit — credit cards, HELOCs in their draw period — has no fixed schedule at all. Minimum payments are recalculated from the current balance, and paying only the minimum can extend repayment for decades.

Reading an amortization schedule

A schedule lists every payment across the life of the loan, with four columns that matter:

  • Interest — that month's charge, calculated as the current balance multiplied by the monthly rate (the annual rate divided by 12).
  • Principal — the payment minus the interest. This is the only part that reduces what you owe.
  • Balance — what remains after the payment. This figure drives next month's interest.
  • Cumulative interest — the running total paid to date. On a mortgage this number is often uncomfortable reading in the early years, and it is the one worth watching.

Two practical uses. If you are considering selling or refinancing, the balance column tells you what you will still owe on a given date — useful for estimating proceeds or a payoff figure. And for a mortgage, the cumulative interest column is the basis of the mortgage-interest deduction if you itemise, though most US filers now take the standard deduction instead.

What this calculator does not include

The output here covers principal and interest only. Real loan payments frequently include more.

A US mortgage payment is usually quoted as PITI: principal, interest, taxes and insurance. Property tax and homeowner's insurance are collected monthly into an escrow account, and if your down payment was under 20%, private mortgage insurance is added on top. These can add several hundred dollars a month and none of it reduces your balance. Escrow amounts also change as tax assessments and premiums are revised, so the total payment moves even on a fixed-rate loan.

Origination fees, points and closing costs are also excluded. Points paid upfront to reduce the rate change the true cost of the loan in a way the monthly payment alone will not reveal — which is why APR, not the headline interest rate, is the figure to compare between lenders. APR folds most lender fees into a single annualised percentage.

Finally, this assumes a fixed rate. On an adjustable-rate mortgage the entire schedule is provisional beyond the fixed period, and rate caps, margins and index behaviour determine what happens next.

Frequently asked questions

Why does most of my early payment go to interest?

Interest is charged on the balance you still owe, which is at its largest when the loan starts. On a $250,000 mortgage at 6.5%, the first payment is $1,354.17 interest and only $226.00 principal. As the balance falls, the interest portion falls with it and more of each payment goes to principal.

When does more of my payment go to principal than interest?

Later than most people expect. On a 30-year mortgage at 6.5%, the crossover falls at payment 233 — year 19.4 of the term. Shorter loans cross over far sooner: a 5-year auto loan at 7% is principal-dominant from the very first payment.

How much does paying extra actually save?

On a $250,000 mortgage at 6.5% over 30 years, an extra $200 per month clears the loan nearly 8 years early and saves $97,618 in interest. Extra payments made early save dramatically more than the same amount paid near the end, because there is more remaining term for the avoided interest to accumulate over.

Is a 15-year mortgage better than a 30-year?

It is cheaper, not automatically better. On $250,000 at 6.5%, the 15-year term costs $598 more each month but saves $176,863 in total interest. The right answer depends on whether the higher payment is comfortable alongside your other commitments — a payment you cannot sustain is a worse outcome than the interest you would have saved.

How much difference does the interest rate make?

On a $250,000 30-year mortgage, moving from 6.5% to 7.5% adds $167.87 per month and $60,432 over the life of the loan. Half a percentage point is worth roughly $30,000. This is why comparing several lenders is worth genuine effort on a mortgage.

Should I compare the interest rate or the APR?

Compare the APR. The interest rate covers only the cost of borrowing the principal, while APR includes most lender fees expressed as an annual percentage. A 6.9% rate with a 3% origination fee is more expensive than a 7.4% rate with no fee, and only the APR makes that visible.

Does this include taxes, insurance and PMI?

No — the figures here are principal and interest only. A US mortgage payment is usually quoted as PITI, adding property tax and homeowner's insurance collected into escrow, plus private mortgage insurance if your down payment was under 20%. These can add several hundred dollars a month and none of it reduces your balance.

Will my lender apply extra payments to principal automatically?

Not always. Some servicers treat additional money as an advance on the next scheduled instalment rather than a principal reduction, which produces none of the interest saving. You usually need to specify that the extra amount goes to principal, and it is worth checking the next statement to confirm it was applied that way.

What Is a Good BMI? BMI Ranges Explained

What is BMI?

Body Mass Index (BMI) is a numerical measure calculated from your height and weight. It was developed by Belgian mathematician Adolphe Quetelet in the 19th century as a population-level measure of weight status — not as a clinical diagnostic tool for individuals.

BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)². In imperial units: BMI = 703 × weight (lb) ÷ height (in)².

BMI ranges for adults

Below 18.5: Underweight — may indicate malnutrition, hormonal issues, or other health concerns. Underweight carries its own health risks.

18.5 to 24.9: Normal weight — the range associated with the lowest risk of weight-related health conditions in population studies.

25.0 to 29.9: Overweight — elevated risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and hypertension, though many individuals in this range are metabolically healthy.

30.0 and above: Obese — significantly elevated risk of multiple chronic conditions. Obesity is further categorised as Class 1 (30–34.9), Class 2 (35–39.9), and Class 3 (40+).

What is a good BMI?

A BMI between 18.5 and 24.9 is generally considered healthy for most adults. However, optimal BMI varies by age, sex, and ethnicity. Research suggests that Asian populations may face elevated health risks at lower BMI thresholds (23+ rather than 25+), and older adults may benefit from a slightly higher BMI than younger adults.

Limitations of BMI

BMI does not distinguish between muscle mass and fat mass. A highly muscular athlete may have a BMI in the 'overweight' range while having very low body fat. Conversely, someone with a 'normal' BMI can have high body fat and low muscle mass (sometimes called 'skinny fat'), which carries its own health risks.

BMI does not account for fat distribution. Abdominal (visceral) fat is metabolically more harmful than fat stored elsewhere. Waist circumference and waist-to-height ratio are better measures of abdominal fat distribution.

For these reasons, BMI is best used as a screening tool rather than a definitive health assessment. Healthcare providers use it alongside other measures including waist circumference, blood pressure, cholesterol, and glucose levels.

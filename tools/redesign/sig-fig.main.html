    Why significant figures matter

    Significant figures record how precisely a quantity was measured. Writing 4.50 cm instead of 4.5 cm claims an extra digit of real precision; a calculated result can never be more precise than the least precise measurement that went into it, so results must be rounded to match.

    The two arithmetic rules

    • Addition & subtraction: round the result to the same number of decimal places as the operand with the fewest decimal places.
    • Multiplication & division: round the result to the same number of significant figures as the operand with the fewest significant figures.
    • Rounding ties: when the digit being dropped is exactly 5 with nothing after it, round to the nearest even digit (half-to-even / banker's rounding), which avoids a systematic upward bias.

    Frequently asked questions

    Does 100 have 1, 2 or 3 significant figures?expand_more

    It is ambiguous as written — by the common convention this calculator uses, trailing zeros with no decimal point are not significant, so "100" reads as 1 sig fig. Writing 100. (with a trailing decimal point) or 1.00 × 10² removes the ambiguity and gives 3 sig figs.

    Are exact numbers and defined constants exempt?expand_more

    Yes. Counting numbers (12 eggs) and exactly defined conversion factors (100 cm = 1 m, by definition) have effectively infinite significant figures and never limit the precision of a calculation.

    Why does half-to-even rounding exist?expand_more

    Always rounding a trailing 5 up introduces a small systematic bias over many roundings. Rounding to the nearest even digit instead splits ties evenly between rounding up and down, keeping large-sample averages unbiased.

    How are significant figures different from decimal places?expand_more

    Decimal places count digits after the decimal point regardless of where they start; significant figures count meaningful digits starting from the first non-zero digit. 0.002 has 3 decimal places but only 1 significant figure.

    What about scientific notation?expand_more

    Scientific notation removes all ambiguity: every digit in the mantissa (the part before ×10ⁿ) is significant by convention, since no digit is needed purely to place the decimal point.

    Does the sig-fig rule apply the same way to subtraction as addition?expand_more

    Yes — subtraction follows the same decimal-places rule as addition. Note that subtracting two close, imprecise numbers can dramatically reduce the number of significant figures in the result (called "catastrophic cancellation").

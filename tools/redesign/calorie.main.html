When a general calorie estimate may not be appropriate

This calculator is a general estimate for adults. Calorie needs can require individualized assessment during pregnancy or breastfeeding, for people under 18, for competitive athletes, or when a medical condition or medication affects nutrition or energy needs. If any of these apply, consider guidance from a qualified healthcare professional.

How this calculator works

This calculator uses the Mifflin-St Jeor equation to estimate your Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR), then multiplies it by an activity factor to estimate total daily energy expenditure — your maintenance calories.

BMR (men) = 10W + 6.25H − 5A + 5 · BMR (women) = 10W + 6.25H − 5A − 161 — W = weight in kg · H = height in cm · A = age in years

The projection: your daily target stays fixed, but each week the calculator recalculates your maintenance calories at your new, lower (or higher) weight. The gap shrinks, so progress slows over time — which is closer to real life than assuming the same weekly loss forever. It doesn't model extra metabolic adaptation, water-weight swings or changes in activity, so treat dates as estimates. For a research-grade model, see the NIH Body Weight Planner, based on work by Kevin Hall and colleagues.

Using this to lose or gain weight

A deficit of roughly 500–550 calories a day tends to produce about 0.5 kg of weight loss per week at first, since one kilogram of body fat corresponds to roughly 7,700 calories (0.5 × 7,700 ÷ 7 = 550 a day). The same logic in reverse applies to weight gain.

Why calorie needs vary so much between people

Two people with identical height, weight, and age can still have meaningfully different calorie needs due to differences in muscle mass, genetics, and daily activity outside of formal exercise (often called NEAT — non-exercise activity thermogenesis).

This calculator provides a solid starting estimate, but the most reliable way to find your true maintenance calories is to track your actual weight trend over 2-3 weeks at a consistent intake and adjust from there based on real results. If you're specifically aiming to lose weight, the Weight Loss Calorie Calculator builds a target deficit on top of this number.

A worked example

A 35-year-old man, 80 kg, 178 cm, lightly active: BMR works out to 10 × 80 + 6.25 × 178 − 5 × 35 + 5 = about 1,743 calories, and multiplying by the 1.375 activity factor gives a maintenance level of about 2,396 calories/day.

For illustration, a calorie target can be set below or above maintenance, but the appropriate size of that change depends on the person and goal. The simple energy-balance model used here should not be treated as a guarantee of a particular weekly weight change.

Why calorie needs change over time

As you lose or gain weight, your calorie needs shift too — a smaller body generally burns fewer calories at rest, and a larger body burns more. This is why weight loss often slows over time even at a consistent deficit, and why recalculating every few weeks (rather than once) tends to produce more accurate ongoing targets.

Body composition can affect energy expenditure, but the effect of changing muscle mass on total calorie needs varies between people. Medical information, not medical advice: talk to a doctor or registered dietitian before a substantial dietary change, especially if you're pregnant, under 18 or managing a health condition.

Activity levels and the multipliers behind them

Your maintenance calories are your BMR multiplied by an activity factor. The calculator uses the five factors below. The last column shows the result for the 35-year-old man in the worked example (80 kg, 178 cm, BMR about 1,743 calories).

Most people choose a level that is too high. If you are unsure between two levels, pick the lower one, track your weight for two to three weeks and adjust.

Mifflin-St Jeor vs Harris-Benedict: how much do the results differ?

Calorie calculators use different BMR equations, so the same person can see different numbers on different sites. The 1919 Harris-Benedict equation, which some calculators still use, tends to give a higher BMR than Mifflin-St Jeor for many adults. Both are estimates.

PersonMifflin-St Jeor BMRHarris-Benedict BMRMaintenance at 1.375 (Mifflin / Harris-Benedict)
Man, 35, 80 kg, 178 cm1,7431,8212,396 / 2,504
Woman, 35, 65 kg, 165 cm1,3451,4181,850 / 1,950

The gap is about 100 calories a day in these examples. That is small next to the error from picking the wrong activity level, which can move the result by 300 calories or more.

Calorie deficit and surplus: what each daily gap does

One kilogram of body weight change is treated as roughly 7,700 calories (about 3,500 per pound). Dividing a daily gap by that figure gives the expected weekly change. The target column uses the example man's maintenance of 2,396.

Daily deficitWeekly changeDaily target for the example
250 caloriesabout 0.23 kg (0.5 lb)2,146
500 caloriesabout 0.45 kg (1 lb)1,896
750 caloriesabout 0.68 kg (1.5 lb)1,646
1,000 caloriesabout 0.91 kg (2 lb)1,396

A surplus works the same way in reverse: adding 300 calories to maintenance suggests about 0.27 kg (0.6 lb) of gain a week, though some of it will be water, and not all of it will be fat. The calculator's projection slows over time because maintenance falls as you lose weight. For a gap above 750 calories, talk to a doctor or dietitian first.

Net calories: food eaten minus exercise burned

Net calories means calories eaten minus calories burned in exercise. If you eat 2,000 calories and burn 350 in a run, your net is 1,650. This calculator already includes exercise in the activity factor, so do not subtract workouts a second time from the target it gives you. Subtract exercise only if you chose the sedentary level and track workouts separately.

Typical daily calorie ranges

The US Dietary Guidelines estimate that adult women need roughly 1,600 to 2,400 calories a day and adult men roughly 2,000 to 3,000, depending on age and activity. Your own number can sit outside those ranges, especially if you are very tall, very small, pregnant or training hard.

How low is too low?

Many dietitians use about 1,200 calories a day for women and 1,500 for men as a floor that should not be passed without medical supervision. Eating that little can make it hard to get enough protein, iron and other nutrients. If your target falls below those figures, choose a slower rate or a longer timeline.

Counting the calories in your food

This calculator tells you how many calories to aim for. It does not look up the calories in individual foods. To use your target, log what you eat in a food diary or a nutrition label database for two weeks, compare the average with your target, and adjust portions. Frequently asked questions

How accurate is this calorie estimate?expand_more

It is a predictive estimate, not a direct measurement of energy expenditure. Individual metabolism and activity vary, so use it as a starting point and compare it with your real-world trend over several weeks.

Why does activity level matter so much?expand_more

Activity level changes the estimate because everyday movement and exercise contribute to total energy expenditure. The size of that difference varies substantially between people.

Why am I not losing weight at my calculated deficit?expand_more

Common causes include underestimating portion sizes, overestimating exercise calories burned, or a maintenance calorie level that's actually different from the formula's estimate — tracking actual results over a few weeks helps calibrate.

Is it safe to eat below my calculated deficit?expand_more

There is no single calorie floor that is appropriate for every adult. Very-low-calorie diets can create nutritional and other health risks and should only be undertaken with appropriate clinical supervision.

Should I recalculate my calorie needs as I lose weight?expand_more

Yes — as body weight changes, so does your BMR and total calorie needs, so recalculating every few weeks during an active weight loss or gain phase keeps your target accurate.

Is a 500-calorie deficit always the right target?expand_more

A fixed 500-calorie deficit is not appropriate for everyone. The size of a deficit should be individualized rather than treated as a guaranteed weekly weight-loss formula.

Why does my projected weight loss slow down over time?expand_more

Because energy needs can change as body weight, body composition and activity change. The projection is a simplified model and should be compared with real-world trends rather than treated as a guaranteed forecast.

What is a calorie calculator and what does it tell me?expand_more

A calorie calculator estimates how many calories you burn in a day (maintenance calories) from your sex, age, height, weight and activity level. It then adjusts that number up or down for your weight goal, so you get a daily calorie target.

How do I calculate a calorie deficit?expand_more

Subtract your target intake from your maintenance calories. For a man with maintenance of 2,396 calories who eats 1,896, the deficit is 500 calories a day. At about 7,700 calories per kilogram, that is roughly 0.45 kg (1 lb) a week.

How many calories should I cut to lose weight?expand_more

A common range is 250 to 750 calories a day below maintenance, which is about 0.2 to 0.7 kg (0.5 to 1.5 lb) a week. A larger cut needs a doctor or dietitian's advice, and your target should not fall under the minimum your health professional recommends.

What is a calorie surplus and how big should it be?expand_more

A calorie surplus is eating more than your maintenance calories. For gradual weight gain, 250 to 500 calories a day above maintenance is typical. Adding 300 calories suggests about 0.27 kg (0.6 lb) a week.

What are net calories?expand_more

Net calories are calories eaten minus calories burned through exercise. If you eat 2,000 and burn 350 exercising, your net is 1,650. This calculator builds exercise into the activity factor, so do not subtract workouts again.

Which activity level should I choose?expand_more

Pick the lowest level that honestly fits your week: 1.2 for little or no exercise, 1.375 for 1–3 workouts, 1.55 for 3–5, 1.725 for 6–7 and 1.9 for a physical job plus training. If unsure, choose lower and adjust after two to three weeks.

Why does another calorie calculator give me a different number?expand_more

Calculators use different equations. This one uses Mifflin-St Jeor. Others use the 1919 Harris-Benedict equation or equations from the Institute of Medicine. For the example man the maintenance figure is 2,396 with Mifflin-St Jeor and 2,504 with Harris-Benedict, about 100 calories apart.

How many calories do men and women need a day?expand_more

The US Dietary Guidelines give roughly 1,600 to 2,400 calories for adult women and 2,000 to 3,000 for adult men, depending on age and activity. Use the calculator for a figure based on your own height and weight.

Is 1,200 calories a day enough?expand_more

Many dietitians treat 1,200 for women and 1,500 for men as a floor not to pass without medical supervision. Below that it is harder to get enough nutrients, so talk to a doctor or dietitian first.

Can I use this calculator to count the calories in my food?expand_more

No. It sets a daily target. To count food calories, log what you eat in a food diary or use nutrition labels, then compare your average intake with the target.

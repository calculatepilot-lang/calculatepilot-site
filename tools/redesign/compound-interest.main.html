Compound Interest Mathematical Specification

The model governing constant-contribution exponential accumulation

A = P · (1 + r/n)nt + PMT · [ ((1 + r/n)nt − 1) / (r/n) ]

Where payments occur at the end of each compounding period. Multiply the contribution term by (1 + r/n) for beginning-of-period deposits.

AFuture Portfolio ValueThe terminal balance after interest compounding.
PInitial PrincipalThe starting capital deposited at inception.
PMTPeriodic ContributionRecurrent cash addition.
r & nRate & CompoundingAnnual interest rate (r) divided by annual frequency (n).
tTenure / HorizonTotal number of years the balance multiplies.

The Compounding Mechanism: Unlike simple interest, where returns are calculated only on the principal (P × r × t), compounding adds each period's interest back to the balance, so later interest is earned on earlier interest.

—

When the contribution frequency differs from the compounding frequency, the calculator converts the rate to the equivalent rate per contribution period, (1 + r/n)n/p − 1. Continuous compounding uses A = Pert.

How to use this compound interest calculator

  1. Enter your starting amount. Enter your initial principal, the one-time amount you start with.
  2. Add regular contributions. Enter your regular contribution, choose how often you pay it, and choose whether it is paid at the end or the beginning of each period.
  3. Set the time horizon. Choose how many years you will invest, from 1 to 50.
  4. Set the return and compounding. Enter the estimated annual return rate and choose how often interest compounds, such as monthly.
  5. Add inflation and read the result. Enter an inflation rate to see real purchasing power, then read your projected balance, total interest and yearly schedule.

APY vs. interest rate — which number to enter

Banks usually advertise APY (annual percentage yield), which already includes compounding. This calculator expects the nominal annual rate before compounding — entering an APY with monthly compounding would count compounding twice and slightly overstate the result. If your bank only quotes an APY, enter it as the rate and choose Annually compounding.

The power of starting early

$300 a month at 7% compounded monthly grows to about $366,000 over 30 years, but only about $156,000 over 20 years. A 10-year head start more than doubles the result while adding just $36,000 of contributions — time is usually the biggest lever in compounding.

A worked example

Start with $8,000, add $250 at the end of each month and earn 7% compounded monthly for 15 years. The balance reaches about $102,000: $53,000 from contributions ($8,000 + $45,000) and roughly $49,000 from compound growth. Stretch it to 25 years and the balance becomes about $248,000 — more than double — even though contributions rise by only $30,000.

Worked example with the calculator defaults

The calculator opens on $25,000 invested, plus $500 added at the end of every month, for 20 years at an 8.0% annual return compounded monthly. Inflation is 2.5%.

  • Total invested: $25,000 + ($500 × 12 × 20) = $145,000.
  • Projected balance after 20 years: $417,680, which is 2.88 times the amount invested.
  • Interest earned: $272,680, which is 65.3% of the final value.
  • Real purchasing power after 2.5% inflation: $254,898 in today's money.

Annual interest passes your annual contributions in year 6. The balance first reaches $50,000 in year 3 and $100,000 in year 8.

The Rule of 72

Divide 72 by the annual rate to estimate how many years money takes to double: at 6% that is about 12 years, at 9% about 8. To model a whole portfolio with fees, see the Investment Calculator; for a fixed savings target, try the Savings Goal Calculator.

What inflation and taxes do to compound growth

The balance the calculator shows is in future dollars. At 3% inflation, $10,000 growing at 5% for 20 years reaches $26,533, but that is worth only about $14,700 in today's purchasing power. Turn on Adjust for Inflation to see the real figure. Interest in a taxable account is also reduced by tax each year, while tax-advantaged accounts such as a 401(k) or Roth IRA let the full balance keep compounding. Use the inflation calculator or the retirement calculator to model it.

Common compound interest mistakes

  • Starting late. Each year of delay removes a year of growth on the entire balance, and the final years are where compounding adds the most.
  • Entering the APY as the rate. APY already includes compounding, so entering it as the interest rate counts the compounding twice.
  • Assuming a steady return. Market returns vary year to year; treat the result as a projection, not a promise.
  • Ignoring fees. A 1% annual fee takes roughly a fifth of a 5% return, and the loss compounds too.
  • Withdrawing early. Every withdrawal removes the interest it would have earned for the rest of the term.

Key takeaways

  • Compound interest earns interest on your interest, so growth speeds up over time. Regular contributions and a longer horizon matter most.
  • On the defaults, $25,000 plus $500 a month at 8% for 20 years grows to about $417,680, and 65.3% of that is interest.
  • Inflation reduces what the balance can buy. At 2.5% inflation, $417,680 has the purchasing power of about $254,898 today.
  • Returns are not guaranteed. Use the calculator to compare scenarios, not to predict results.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between simple and compound interest?expand_more

Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal. Compound interest is calculated on the principal plus any interest already earned, so it grows faster over time.

How often should interest compound for the best results?expand_more

More frequent compounding (daily or monthly) produces slightly higher returns than annual compounding at the same nominal rate, though the difference is usually small.

Should I enter my bank's APY or interest rate?expand_more

Enter the interest rate before compounding. If only an APY is listed, enter it as the rate with annual compounding so compounding isn't counted twice.

What's a realistic compound growth rate to assume?expand_more

Historical long-term stock market averages run around 7-10% before inflation, though returns vary significantly year to year and aren't guaranteed. Savings accounts pay a variable rate that follows central-bank rates.

Does this calculator account for inflation?expand_more

Yes, optionally. With Adjust for Inflation on, the Real Purchasing Power figure divides the final balance by (1 + inflation)years to show its value in today's money.

Does compounding frequency matter much?expand_more

Less than most people think. At 5% for 10 years, $10,000 grows to $16,289 compounded annually, $16,470 monthly and $16,487 daily — the rate and time invested matter far more.

What is the Rule of 72?expand_more

A quick mental shortcut: divide 72 by your annual growth rate to estimate how many years it takes an investment to double. At 8% growth, that's about 9 years.

How much is $25,000 worth after 20 years at 8%?expand_more

With $500 added every month and monthly compounding, $25,000 grows to about $417,680 in 20 years. You contribute $145,000 and earn $272,680 of interest.

What is the value after inflation?expand_more

At 2.5% inflation, a $417,680 balance has the purchasing power of about $254,898 in today’s money.

When does compound interest overtake my contributions?expand_more

On the defaults, annual interest exceeds your annual contributions in year 6. The balance first passes $50,000 in year 3 and $100,000 in year 8.

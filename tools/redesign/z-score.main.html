    Population vs sample z-scores

    The z-score formula is the same either way; what changes is where the mean and standard deviation come from. Use σ when you know the whole population, and the sample statistics when you only have a sample.

    Population z-score μ and σ known

    Use when the mean and standard deviation describe the entire population — for example a published test norm.

    z = (x − μ) ÷ σ

    Sample z-score x̄ and s from data

    Use when the mean x̄ and standard deviation s are estimated from a sample. s divides by n − 1 (Bessel's correction).

    z = (x − x̄) ÷ s

    Worked example: a standardised test score

    A test has a mean of 500 and a standard deviation of 100, and results are roughly normally distributed. A candidate scores 650. How did they do relative to everyone else?

    1. 1

      State what you know

      Raw score x = 650, mean μ = 500, standard deviation σ = 100.

    2. 2

      Subtract the mean

      How far the score sits from the average.

      x − μ = 650 − 500 = +150
    3. 3

      Divide by the standard deviation

      Express that distance in standard deviations.

      z = 150 ÷ 100 = +1.50
    4. 4

      Read off the percentile

      The standard normal CDF at 1.50 is 0.9332, so the candidate scored higher than about 93.3% of test-takers.

      Φ(1.50) = 0.9332 → 93.32nd percentile

    What is a z-score?

    A z-score (standard score) says how many standard deviations a value lies from the mean of its distribution. A z-score of 0 is exactly average, +1 is one standard deviation above, and −2 is two below. Because the result has no units, z-scores let you compare a height in centimetres with an exam mark or a lab value.

    How to calculate a z-score

    z = (x − μ) ÷ σ, where x is the raw score, μ the mean and σ the standard deviation. To go the other way, x = μ + zσ. The percentile comes from the standard normal cumulative distribution function Φ(z), assuming the data is approximately normal.

    • Left tail = Φ(z), the share of values below x.
    • Right tail = 1 − Φ(z), the share above x.
    • Two-tailed = 2 × the smaller tail, the share at least as far from the mean in either direction.

    Interpreting your result

    Between −1 and +1 is ordinary variation, and beyond ±2 is unusual: roughly 5% of a normal distribution lies that far out. Beyond ±3 is rare (about 0.27%). The percentile is only meaningful if the data is close to bell-shaped — for skewed data a high z-score does not mean the same tail probability.

    Frequently asked questions

    What does a negative z-score mean?expand_more

    It means the value is below the mean. A z-score of −1.5 is one and a half standard deviations below average, which on a normal distribution is about the 6.68th percentile.

    How do I convert a z-score to a percentile?expand_more

    Evaluate the standard normal cumulative distribution function Φ(z) and multiply by 100. For example Φ(1.00) = 0.8413, so z = 1 is the 84.13th percentile. This calculator does that for you and also shows both tail areas.

    What is the difference between a z-score and a t-score?expand_more

    A z-score uses a known population standard deviation and the normal distribution. A t statistic uses a standard deviation estimated from a small sample and the t distribution, which has heavier tails. Separately, "T-score" in psychometrics means a standardised score with mean 50 and standard deviation 10, which is unrelated to the t distribution.

    What are the critical z-values for 95% and 99% confidence?expand_more

    For a two-sided interval, z = 1.960 for 95% confidence and z = 2.576 for 99% confidence. For 90% it is 1.645. Use the inverse mode with a percentile of 97.5 or 99.5 to reproduce them.

    Should I use the sample or the population standard deviation?expand_more

    Use the population standard deviation (divide by N) only when your data is the whole population. If it is a sample drawn from a larger group, divide by n − 1 to get the unbiased estimate of the variance, which is the usual choice.

    Does the percentile work for any data?expand_more

    No. The z-score itself is always valid, but the percentile and tail areas assume an approximately normal distribution. For strongly skewed or heavy-tailed data they can be badly off.

    How the weighted average is derived

    A simple mean treats every observation as equally important. The weighted mean lets each observation count in proportion to its weight, so a heavy item moves the result more than a light one.

    The formula

    Weighted arithmetic meanx̄w = Σ(wᵢ · xᵢ) ÷ Σwᵢ

    Multiply each value xᵢ by its weight wᵢ, add those products, then divide by the sum of the weights. Dividing by Σw is what makes the result a true average whatever units the weights use.

    When weights are percentages

    Normalised weightsΣwᵢ = 100% (or 1.00)

    If the weights already total 100%, the denominator is 1 and the weighted mean is simply Σ(wᵢ · xᵢ) with each weight written as a fraction. If they do not total 100%, dividing by Σw rescales them automatically.

    Two worked examples

    Both are checked by hand, and both can be reproduced above (the second uses a different weight scale).

    Course grade percent weights

    A student scores 78 on homework (20%), 85 on the midterm (30%) and 92 on the final (50%).

    Σ(w·x) = (0.20 × 78) + (0.30 × 85) + (0.50 × 92)
       = 15.6 + 25.5 + 46.0 = 87.1
    Σw = 0.20 + 0.30 + 0.50 = 1.00
    Weighted average = 87.10 ÷ 1.00 = 87.10

    Contrast: the simple average is (78 + 85 + 92) ÷ 3 = 85.00. The heavy final exam lifts the result by 2.10 points.

    Portfolio return dollar weights

    $50,000 earns 12%, $30,000 earns 8% and $20,000 earns 4%.

    Σ(w·x) = (50,000 × 12) + (30,000 × 8) + (20,000 × 4)
       = 600,000 + 240,000 + 80,000 = 920,000
    Σw = 100,000
    Weighted return = 920,000 ÷ 100,000 = 9.20%

    Contrast: the simple average is (12 + 8 + 4) ÷ 3 = 8.00%. The largest holding earns the most, so the portfolio return is 1.20 points higher.

    What is a weighted average?

    A weighted average (weighted arithmetic mean) is an average in which each value counts according to an assigned weight. It is the right tool when some observations represent more of the total than others.

    How to calculate a weighted average

    • Multiply each value by its weight.
    • Add the products together.
    • Add the weights together.
    • Divide the sum of products by the sum of weights.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is the difference between a simple mean and a weighted average?expand_more

    A simple mean gives every value the same importance: add and divide by the count. A weighted average lets each value count in proportion to its weight, so items with larger weights influence the result more.

    What happens if the weights do not add up to 100?expand_more

    Nothing goes wrong: the calculation divides by the total of the weights, so it rescales them automatically. For example weights of 2, 3 and 5 give the same result as 20%, 30% and 50%. This page flags a total other than 100 only so you can check for a typing slip.

    Can weights be negative?expand_more

    Mathematically the formula still works, but negative weights are unusual and can push the result outside the range of the values. In grading and finance weights are normally zero or positive, and this page warns you if any weight is negative.

    How does a zero weight affect the result?expand_more

    A zero-weight item contributes nothing to the numerator or the denominator, so it does not change the weighted average. It still counts in the simple average shown for comparison.

    How do I calculate a GPA with this?expand_more

    Enter each course's grade points (for example A = 4.0) as the value and its credit hours as the weight. The weighted average is your GPA on that scale. Check your institution's own grade-point conversion.

    What if the sum of weights is zero?expand_more

    The average is undefined because the formula would divide by zero. Give at least one item a non-zero weight.

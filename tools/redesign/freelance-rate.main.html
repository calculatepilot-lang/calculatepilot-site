    receipt_longSelf-Employment Tax & Schedule C Deductions
    
    
    Employees split FICA with their employer. As a 1099 contractor or single-member LLC, you pay both halves — 15.3% self-employment (SECA) tax:
    
    
      
      
    • check_circle12.4% Social Security: on net self-employment earnings up to the $184,500 wage base for 2026.
      • 
      
    • check_circle2.9% Medicare: no cap, plus an additional 0.9% above $200,000 (single filers).
      • 
      
    • check_circle92.35% rule & half deduction: SE tax applies to 92.35% of net profit, and you deduct half of it when figuring income tax.
      •

    Good for sprint-based design, technical audits and advisory blocks — no minute-by-minute time tracking.
    
      
    Monthly Retainers
    Predictable revenue for ongoing work, such as embedded engineering or product strategy support.
    
    
    
  
    

    


    
  
    
    
    How this calculator works
    
    
    It works backwards from your take-home goal. Net profit is revenue minus your margin buffer and business expenses. Self-employment tax is 15.3% of 92.35% of that profit (Social Security capped at the 2026 wage base of $184,500). Income tax is your effective rate applied to profit minus half the SE tax, health insurance premiums and pre-tax retirement contributions. The calculator finds the revenue that leaves exactly your target after all of that, then divides it by your billable hours.
    
    
    By Suhaib Hassan · Formula verified 27 Sep 2026 · How CalculatePilot verifies formulas →
    

    
    Why billable hours are lower than total working hours
    
    
    This is the single biggest mistake freelancers make when setting rates — assuming all working hours are billable. In reality, time spent on invoicing, marketing, client communication, admin, and finding new work isn't billable, which is why this calculator applies a billable realization rate to your total working hours.
    
    
    Many freelancers find that only 50-70% of their total working time ends up being billable once non-client work is accounted for — using your total working hours instead of realistic billable hours will significantly undercharge you.
    

    
    Why taxes and expenses both matter here
    
    
    As a freelancer, you're typically responsible for self-employment tax and income tax that an employer would otherwise partially cover or withhold automatically — factoring them in ensures your target 'take-home' income is actually achievable after taxes, not before.
    
    
    Business expenses (software subscriptions, equipment, home office costs, insurance) also need to be covered by your rate — since these come out of revenue before you see any personal income, forgetting to include them means your actual take-home will fall short of your goal.
    

    
    Frequently asked questions
    
    
    Why isn't my hourly rate just my income goal divided by total hours worked?expand_more
    Because not all working hours are billable to clients — time spent on admin, marketing, and finding new work reduces your actual billable hours below your total working hours, so your rate needs to be higher to compensate.
    
    
    Should I include taxes in my rate calculation?expand_more
    Yes — freelancers are typically responsible for self-employment and income tax that isn't withheld automatically, so your target take-home income needs to be grossed up to account for taxes you'll owe.

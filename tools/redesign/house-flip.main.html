    functionsThe 70% Rule & Maximum Allowable Offer (MAO)

    Many flippers use the 70% rule as a quick screen: pay no more than 70% of the after-repair value minus the repair budget.

    Why 70%? The remaining 30% of ARV is meant to cover buying and selling costs, financing and holding costs, overruns, and still leave a profit. In expensive or slow markets the right percentage may differ.

    scheduleHolding Cost Erosion & Delay Sensitivity

    Time is one of the biggest risks in a flip. Interest and carrying costs run every month the property is unsold, and permit or contractor delays add up quickly.

    How to use this house flip calculator

    1. Enter the ARV and purchase price. Enter the target after-repair value (ARV) and your target purchase price, plus buyer closing costs.
    2. Add the rehab budget and timeline. Enter the rehab costs by category and the number of months the project will take.
    3. Set the financing. Choose how the deal is financed, then enter the loan-to-cost ratio, interest rate and points if you borrow.
    4. Add holding and selling costs. Enter monthly property taxes, insurance, utilities and HOA fees, plus the broker commission, transfer taxes and buyer concessions.
    5. Read the profit and the 70% rule check. Read your net profit, ROI and cash invested, and check your purchase price against the maximum allowable offer.

    Worked example with the calculator defaults

    You buy a house for $280,000 and expect an ARV of $450,000. Rehab is $60,000, the project takes 6 months, and a hard-money loan covers 85% of cost at 11.5% with 2 points.

    • Acquisition: $280,000 plus 1.5% buyer closing costs = $284,200.
    • Rehab: $60,000. Financing: $22,398. Carrying costs: $5,100.
    • Selling costs: $32,750 (commission, transfer taxes and $3,500 of concessions).
    • Total project outlay: $404,448. Net profit: $45,553, which is 10.1% of the ARV.

    The 70% rule gives a maximum allowable offer of ($450,000 × 70%) − $60,000 = $255,000. The $280,000 purchase price is $25,000 above that, so this deal needs tight execution.

    What holding costs cover

    Holding costs are often underestimated by new flippers. They include property taxes, insurance, utilities, loan interest if the purchase is financed, and HOA fees. All of them accrue between purchase and sale. The longer a renovation takes, the more they add up. Timeline overruns can quietly erode profit even when the renovation budget stays on track.

    Build a realistic timeline buffer into your holding cost estimate rather than assuming a best-case schedule. It tends to produce a more reliable profit projection.

    Why accurate ARV estimation matters most

    The after-repair value (ARV) is typically the most sensitive number in this calculation. It estimates a future sale price rather than a known figure. An overly optimistic ARV can make a marginal deal look profitable on paper when it isn't. Base your ARV on recent comparable sales in the immediate area, ideally reviewed by a local agent or appraiser. That gives a more reliable projection than a rough guess.

    Selling costs (agent commissions, closing costs and staging) commonly run 8-10% of the sale price. They are easy to underestimate. Always build them into your profit calculation rather than treating the full ARV as available profit.

    Key takeaways

    • House flip profit is the ARV minus purchase, rehab, financing, holding and selling costs. Your ROI depends on how much cash you put in.
    • The 70% rule caps your offer at 70% of ARV minus rehab. On the defaults that is $255,000, and the $280,000 price is above it.
    • On the defaults the net profit is $45,553, or 10.1% of ARV. A common target is 10% or more.
    • The ARV and the timeline move profit the most. Check comparable sales and add a buffer to the schedule.

    Frequently asked questions

    What's typically included in selling costs?expand_more

    Real estate agent commissions, closing costs, and often staging or minor prep costs — commonly totaling around 8-10% of the sale price, though this varies by market and negotiated agent rates.

    Why is ARV the most important number to get right?expand_more

    Because it's an estimate of a future sale price rather than a known figure, an overly optimistic ARV can make an unprofitable deal look attractive on paper — basing it on solid recent comparable sales data significantly improves the reliability of the whole projection.

    What is the 70% rule in house flipping?expand_more

    It says to pay no more than 70% of the after-repair value minus the repair budget. On the calculator defaults, $450,000 × 70% − $60,000 = $255,000. The $280,000 purchase price is $25,000 above that maximum allowable offer.

    How much profit should I make on a flip?expand_more

    A common target is 10% of the ARV or more. On the defaults the net profit is $45,553, which is 10.1% of the $450,000 ARV.

    How much do holding costs add up to?expand_more

    On the defaults, financing and carrying costs total $27,498 over 6 months: $22,398 of loan interest and points plus $5,100 of taxes, insurance and utilities. Each extra month adds about $3,620.

    What is ARV in real estate?expand_more

    ARV is the after-repair value, the price you expect the house to sell for once the renovation is done. Base it on recent comparable sales close to the property.

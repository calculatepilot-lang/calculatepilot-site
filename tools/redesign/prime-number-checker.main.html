    What is a prime number?

    A prime number is a whole number greater than 1 with exactly two positive divisors: 1 and itself. Every other whole number greater than 1 is composite, and by the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, every composite number factors uniquely into primes.

    How the tests work

    • Trial division: test divisibility by every integer up to √n; if none divide evenly, n is prime.
    • Miller–Rabin (deterministic here): a fast primality test that, using a fixed, proven set of witness bases, gives an exact yes/no answer for any n below 2⁶⁴ — no randomness or uncertainty involved.
    • Lucas–Lehmer: for Mersenne numbers Mₚ = 2ᵖ − 1 with p prime, iterate s ← s² − 2 (mod Mₚ) starting from s = 4; Mₚ is prime exactly when the result is 0 after p − 2 steps.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is 1 a prime number?expand_more

    No. By definition a prime has exactly two distinct positive divisors; 1 has only one (itself), so it is classified as neither prime nor composite.

    Why does trial division only need to check up to √n?expand_more

    If n = a × b with a ≤ b, then a ≤ √n. So if no divisor up to √n is found, no divisor beyond √n can exist either — every composite number has at least one factor at or below its square root.

    What makes Carmichael numbers tricky?expand_more

    A Carmichael number is composite but satisfies Fermat's little theorem for every base coprime to it, so the simple Fermat primality test wrongly calls it prime. Miller–Rabin with proper witnesses correctly identifies it as composite.

    What is a Mersenne prime?expand_more

    A prime of the form 2ᵖ − 1. p itself must be prime (a necessary but not sufficient condition) — for example, 2¹¹ − 1 = 2047 = 23 × 89 is composite even though 11 is prime. The largest known primes today are almost all Mersenne primes, found via the Lucas–Lehmer test.

    How many primes are there?expand_more

    Infinitely many — proved by Euclid around 300 BCE: assuming a finite list of primes and multiplying them together plus 1 always produces a number with a prime factor not on the list, a contradiction.

    Why are primes important in cryptography?expand_more

    RSA encryption relies on the fact that multiplying two large primes is fast, but factoring their product back into those primes is computationally hard — this asymmetry is what keeps the encryption secure.

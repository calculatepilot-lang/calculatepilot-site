The 40x Rule vs the 30% Guideline

Many landlords in expensive cities — New York especially — require your annual income to be at least 40 times the monthly rent. That works out to rent of 30% of gross monthly income, the same as the classic guideline, but landlords apply it as a hard cut-off.

If you fall short, landlords often accept a guarantor earning around 80 times the rent, or an institutional guarantor service that charges a fee — often in the range of one month's rent.

The Hidden Costs of Renting

The advertised rent is rarely the whole monthly bill. Budget for:

  • Building fees: some properties charge mandatory amenity, trash or parking fees.
  • Utilities & insurance: electricity, internet, and renters insurance.
  • Broker fees: in some markets tenants pay a fee; New York City's FARE Act (2025) generally stops landlords passing on the fee for a broker they hired.

The 30% rule

Spending no more than 30% of gross income on rent is a commonly cited affordability guideline. It is a rule of thumb, not a law. Your comfortable number depends on other debts, savings goals and cost of living.

Beyond the 30% rule

The 30% guideline is a useful starting point. Your comfortable rent budget also depends on existing debt payments, savings goals, commute costs and local cost of living. Someone with no debt and low other expenses might comfortably afford more than 30%. Someone with significant student loans or car payments may want to budget less.

Also factor in renter's insurance, utilities and moving costs when you budget for a new place. The advertised rent is rarely the complete monthly housing cost.

How to use this rent calculator

  1. Enter your income and debts. Enter your gross annual household income and your total monthly debt payments.
  2. Set your tax rate. Enter your effective tax rate so the calculator can estimate your monthly take-home pay.
  3. Choose a rent standard. Choose the conservative 25% of net pay, the standard 30% of gross income, the landlord 40x rule, or your own custom percentage.
  4. Add move-in costs. Enter the security deposit, any broker fee you pay, monthly utilities and renters insurance, and application and moving costs.
  5. Read your maximum rent. Read your recommended maximum rent, your total debt burden, your monthly cash flow and the cash you need to move in.

A worked example

At $6,200/month gross income with a 30% target, the recommended maximum rent is $1,860/month, or about $22,320 annually.

If that same person budgeted 25% instead, perhaps because of other debt, the maximum rent drops to $1,550 a month. That is a $310 difference that could go toward savings or debt payoff.

Worked example with the calculator defaults

Your household income is $85,000 a year, with $550 a month of debt payments and a 25% effective tax rate. Utilities and renters insurance cost $220 a month.

  • 30% rule: $85,000 ÷ 12 × 30% = $2,125 a month ($25,500 a year).
  • Landlord 40x rule: $85,000 ÷ 40 = $2,125, so you qualify at that rent.
  • Conservative 25% of net pay: $1,328 a month.
  • True monthly housing cost: $2,125 + $220 = $2,345.

Take-home pay is about $5,313 a month. After $2,345 for housing and $550 of debt, about $2,418 (45.5%) is left for everything else and savings. Your total burden of rent plus debt is 37.8% of gross income. Move-in cash is $4,570: first month's rent $2,125, a one-month deposit $2,125 and $320 for application and moving costs.

Other costs of renting to budget for

Beyond the base rent, renters typically budget for utilities, renters insurance, parking and sometimes pet fees. All of these add to your effective monthly housing cost. The 30% guideline alone does not capture them.

In competitive rental markets, landlords may also require proof of income at a multiple of monthly rent, commonly 2.5-3x. That is a related but separate calculation from the affordability guideline this calculator uses.

Key takeaways

  • How much rent you can afford is commonly set at no more than 30% of gross income. Some landlords instead use a 40x rule: annual income divided by 40.
  • On the defaults ($85,000 income), the maximum rent is $2,125 a month under both the 30% rule and the 40x rule.
  • Rent is not the full cost. Add utilities, renters insurance and your debt payments, and keep cash for move-in costs.
  • These are guidelines, not rules. Your comfortable number depends on your debts, savings goals and local costs.

Frequently asked questions

Is 30% the right target for everyone?expand_more

It's a widely used guideline, but people with significant other debts may want to budget less, while those with minimal other expenses may comfortably afford more.

What if 30% of my income isn't enough rent for my area?expand_more

In high cost-of-living areas, many renters exceed the 30% guideline out of necessity — if so, it's worth budgeting extra carefully elsewhere and building a larger emergency fund to compensate.

Should I include roommates' contributions in this calculation?expand_more

No — calculate based on your own income and your own share of the rent, to get an accurate picture of what you personally can afford.

Do landlords typically require a minimum income relative to rent?expand_more

Many landlords require monthly income of roughly 2.5-3x the rent amount as a qualifying condition, which is a common industry practice separate from personal affordability guidelines.

How much rent can I afford on $85,000 a year?expand_more

Under the 30% rule it is $2,125 a month, which is $85,000 ÷ 12 × 30%. The landlord 40x rule gives the same $2,125. A conservative cap of 25% of take-home pay comes to $1,328 a month.

What is the 40x rule for rent?expand_more

Many landlords in expensive cities, especially New York, ask that your annual income is at least 40 times the monthly rent. The maximum rent is then annual income divided by 40. At $85,000 that is $2,125 a month.

How much cash do I need to move into a rental?expand_more

On the calculator defaults, about $4,570: first month’s rent of $2,125, a one-month security deposit of $2,125, and $320 for application and moving costs. Broker fees add to that where they apply.

How this calculator works

The U.S. Navy method estimates body fat from circumference measurements using a logarithmic formula, and is a widely used field method that doesn't require calipers or special equipment.

  • Navy — men (cm): 495 ÷ (1.0324 − 0.19077 × log₁₀(waist − neck) + 0.15456 × log₁₀(height)) − 450
  • Navy — women (cm): 495 ÷ (1.29579 − 0.35004 × log₁₀(waist + hip − neck) + 0.22100 × log₁₀(height)) − 450
  • Jackson-Pollock 3-site: body density from the sum of three skinfolds and age (men: chest, abdomen, thigh; women: triceps, suprailiac, thigh), converted to body fat with the Siri equation: 495 ÷ density − 450.
  • Deurenberg (BMI-based): 1.20 × BMI + 0.23 × age − 10.8 × sex (1 = male, 0 = female) − 5.4. A rough population estimate, shown for comparison only.

How to make repeat measurements more useful

  • Use the same measurement method and locations each time.
  • Measure under similar conditions, such as a similar time of day.
  • Keep the tape snug without compressing the skin.
  • Repeat measurements when practical and look at the trend rather than one isolated percentage.

Consistent technique makes changes over time easier to interpret, even though the absolute percentage remains an estimate.

Accuracy of this method

The Navy method can be useful for consistent at-home tracking, but the size and direction of its error vary between people and it should not be presented as interchangeable with DEXA. Skinfold results also depend heavily on measurement technique and operator skill.

Comparing measurement methods

Besides the Navy circumference and skinfold methods used here, other common body fat estimation methods include bioelectrical impedance scales and DEXA scans. DEXA is generally considered the most accurate but requires specialized equipment, while circumference and caliper methods offer a reasonable free or low-cost alternative.

For tracking progress over time, consistency in measurement method and technique matters more than absolute accuracy — comparing this month's Navy method result to last month's using the same technique is more meaningful than comparing to a one-time DEXA scan result.

A worked example

A man with a 90 cm waist, 38 cm neck, and 178 cm height: using the Navy formula, this works out to about 20% body fat, in the "average" ACE category.

If his waist measured 100 cm instead (all else equal), the estimate rises to about 27% — illustrating how sensitive this method is to waist measurement specifically, making consistent, careful measurement technique important for tracking change over time.

Fat mass, lean mass and target weight

Fat mass = weight × body fat %. Lean mass = weight − fat mass. To see what you would weigh at a lower percentage while keeping the same lean mass, divide lean mass by (1 − target %). Example for an 80 kg person at 25%: fat mass 20 kg, lean mass 60 kg.

This is a simple estimate. In practice some lean mass is usually lost along with fat, so recalculate as you go and treat the results as a guide, not a goal for any individual.

Frequently asked questions

How accurate is the U.S. Navy body fat method?expand_more

It is a field estimate and measurement error can be meaningful. Accuracy varies with body composition, measurement technique and the method used; it should not be treated as equivalent to a clinical body-composition measurement.

Why does the formula differ for men and women?expand_more

Body fat distribution patterns differ by sex, so the formula includes hip measurement for women to account for typical fat distribution differences.

What is a healthy body fat percentage?expand_more

Reference ranges for body fat vary by age, sex, population and source. These categories are educational reference points, not personalized medical targets or diagnoses.

How often should I re-measure my body fat?expand_more

Monthly is a reasonable cadence for tracking meaningful change — body fat percentage doesn't shift dramatically week to week, so frequent measurement mostly captures measurement noise rather than real change.

How does the Navy method compare to a DEXA scan?expand_more

DEXA and circumference methods use different measurement approaches. A DEXA result should not be treated as a simple conversion or guaranteed ground truth for an individual; the Navy result is best used as a consistent field estimate.

Where exactly should I measure my waist and neck?expand_more

Waist is typically measured at the navel level, and neck just below the larynx — consistent placement each time you measure matters more than the exact spot, for tracking change over time.

Tape measure or skinfold calipers — which is better?expand_more

Both are estimates. Skinfolds taken by a trained person can be quite accurate, but results vary a lot with technique; the tape method is easier to repeat consistently at home. Pick one and use it the same way each time.

How do I calculate fat mass and lean mass from body fat percentage?expand_more

Fat mass is your weight multiplied by your body fat percentage, and lean mass is what remains. At 80 kg and 25%, fat mass is 20 kg and lean mass is 60 kg.

What would I weigh at a lower body fat percentage?expand_more

Divide your lean mass by (1 minus the target percentage). With 60 kg of lean mass, 20% body fat is 60 ÷ 0.80 = 75 kg. This assumes lean mass stays the same, which is rarely exactly true, so use it as a rough guide.

Is body fat percentage better than BMI?expand_more

They answer different questions. BMI only uses height and weight, so it cannot tell muscle from fat. Body fat percentage separates fat from lean mass, but its estimates have their own error, so many people track both.

Why do the Navy and skinfold methods give different results?expand_more

They measure different things: the Navy method infers fat from circumferences and height, while skinfolds estimate body density from fat folds under the skin. Each has its own error, so compare results from the same method over time.

Is the Navy method reliable for very muscular people?expand_more

It is less reliable. Waist and neck circumferences reflect muscle as well as fat, so the formula can misjudge people whose build differs from the population it was based on. Use it for tracking trends rather than as an exact figure.

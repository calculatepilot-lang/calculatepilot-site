Underwriting Rule: 28/36 Standard

menu_bookThe 28/36 Underwriting Rule Explained

Lenders commonly use two debt-to-income ratios to decide how large a mortgage payment you can take on:

  • check_circleFront-End Ratio (≤ 28%): no more than 28% of gross monthly income should go to housing costs — principal, interest, property taxes and insurance (PITI), plus PMI.
  • check_circleBack-End Ratio (≤ 36%): total monthly debt payments — the new housing payment plus student loans, credit cards and auto loans — should stay within 36% of gross income.
Formula
Max PITI = min(Gross × 0.28, Gross × 0.36 − Debts)

The maximum price is the one whose full monthly cost — P&I on the loan, property tax, insurance and any PMI — exactly equals that allowed payment, with P&I from the standard fixed-rate loan formula.

How lenders apply the 28/36 rule

Lenders commonly use two ratios to judge affordability. The front-end ratio caps housing costs at 28% of gross monthly income. The back-end ratio caps total debt payments, housing plus all other debts, at 36%. This calculator uses whichever limit is more restrictive for you.

These are guidelines, not hard rules. Some lenders allow higher back-end ratios, up to 43-50%, for well-qualified borrowers with strong credit and stable income. More conservative buyers may prefer to stay well under these limits.

How to use this house affordability calculator

  1. Enter income and debts. Enter your annual gross household income and your total monthly debt payments, such as car loans, student loans and credit cards.
  2. Enter your down payment. Enter the cash you have available for a down payment. A down payment under 20% adds PMI.
  3. Set the rate and term. Enter the mortgage interest rate and choose the loan term. The default is a 30-year term.
  4. Add taxes, insurance and PMI. Enter the property tax rate, annual homeowners insurance and the PMI rate that applies if you put down less than 20%.
  5. Choose a standard and read the result. Pick the underwriting standard (conventional 28/36, FHA 31/43 or aggressive 36/45) and read your maximum home price and monthly payment.

Worked example with the calculator defaults

Take a household income of $120,000, $60,000 for a down payment, $550 of monthly debts, a 6.50% rate and a 30-year loan. Property tax is 1.20% of the price, insurance is $1,200 a year and PMI is 0.50%.

  • Front-end limit: $120,000 ÷ 12 × 28% = $2,800 a month for housing.
  • Back-end limit: $10,000 × 36% − $550 of debts = $3,050 a month for housing.
  • The front-end limit is lower, so it sets the payment cap at $2,800.
  • Maximum home price: about $401,000 with a $341,000 loan and a 15.0% down payment.

That $2,800 monthly payment is about $2,157 of principal and interest, $401 of property tax, $100 of insurance and $142 of PMI.

What this estimate doesn't include

Property tax is applied as a percentage of the home price. Insurance is a yearly amount you enter. PMI is added at your chosen rate whenever the down payment is under 20%. Rates differ by state and even by county, so use your local figures.

It doesn't include HOA fees, closing costs or your personal comfort level with a payment. Getting pre-approved by a lender is still the most accurate way to know your real number.

Key takeaways

  • Your maximum home price is the price whose full monthly cost fits inside the lower of your front-end (28%) and back-end (36%) limits.
  • On the default example ($120,000 income, $60,000 down, 6.50% for 30 years), the max home price is about $401,000.
  • More monthly debt lowers the back-end limit. A larger down payment, a lower rate or a longer term raises your price.
  • This is a planning estimate, not a pre-approval. HOA fees and closing costs are not included.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my back-end ratio lower than my front-end limit?expand_more

The back-end ratio subtracts your existing monthly debts (car payments, student loans, credit cards) from your total allowed debt load, which often makes it the more restrictive limit if you carry other debt.

Does this account for property taxes and insurance?expand_more

Yes — enter your property tax rate and annual homeowners insurance, and PMI is added automatically when you put down less than 20%. All of them count toward the housing payment used in both ratios.

What is the 28/36 rule?expand_more

It is a lending guideline. Housing costs should be no more than 28% of gross monthly income, and total debt payments should be no more than 36%. Some lenders allow higher ratios for well-qualified borrowers.

How much house can I afford on a $120,000 income?expand_more

With the calculator defaults ($120,000 income, $60,000 down payment, $550 of monthly debts, 6.50% rate and a 30-year term), the maximum home price is about $401,000. Your own number changes with your debts, down payment, rate and local taxes.

Does a bigger down payment raise how much I can afford?expand_more

Yes. The monthly payment cap stays the same, so a bigger down payment lowers the loan and lets the price rise. Below 20% down, PMI is added. At 20% down or more it drops out.

Is this the same as a mortgage pre-approval?expand_more

No. This is a planning estimate based on standard ratios. A lender also checks your credit, employment, assets and loan program rules. Get pre-approved to learn your real number.

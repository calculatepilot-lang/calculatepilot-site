How this calculator works

This uses the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, a widely used general-population predictive equation. No BMR equation is universally most accurate for every person.

Men: 10W + 6.25H − 5A + 5 · Women: 10W + 6.25H − 5A − 161 — W = weight in kg · H = height in cm · A = age in years

For comparison it also shows the revised Harris-Benedict equation (Roza & Shizgal, 1984) and, if you enter your body-fat percentage, the lean-mass-based Katch-McArdle (370 + 21.6 × LBM) and Cunningham (500 + 22 × LBM) equations.

BMR vs. total daily energy expenditure

BMR is just your resting energy use — it doesn't include activity. Multiply BMR by an activity factor (roughly 1.2 to 1.9) to estimate your total daily calorie needs, which is what the range above shows as rough bounds. For a more precise activity-adjusted number, use our TDEE Calculator.

BMR changes over your lifetime

BMR generally decreases with age, partly due to natural muscle loss over time (since muscle burns more calories at rest than fat tissue) — this is one reason calorie needs often decrease gradually as people get older even without weight changes.

Maintaining muscle mass through resistance training may help preserve resting energy expenditure as body composition changes with age, although individual changes vary.

A worked example

A 28-year-old woman, 65kg, 165cm: BMR works out to 10 times 65 plus 6.25 times 165 minus 5 times 28 minus 161, which equals 650 plus 1,031 minus 140 minus 161, or 1,380 calories/day at complete rest.

At a sedentary activity level (times 1.2), her estimated daily calorie needs would be about 1,656; at a very active level (times 1.725), that rises to roughly 2,381 — a difference of over 700 calories purely from activity level.

Factors that influence your BMR beyond this formula

While the Mifflin-St Jeor equation is widely used for general-purpose estimation, individual resting energy expenditure can vary due to genetics, muscle mass, hormone levels (including thyroid function), and even body temperature regulation — meaning your actual BMR could differ somewhat from the formula's estimate. How to calculate BMR by hand

Example: a 35-year-old man, 80 kg, 180 cm, with the Mifflin-St Jeor equation.

  • 10 × 80 = 800
  • 6.25 × 180 = 1,125
  • 5 × 35 = 175
  • 800 + 1,125 − 175 + 5 = 1,755 kcal a day

Multiplying by 1.2 (sedentary) gives about 2,106 kcal and by 1.55 (moderately active) about 2,720 kcal.

Mifflin-St Jeor vs revised Harris-Benedict

Muscle, fat and your BMR

Published estimates put the resting energy use of skeletal muscle at roughly 13 kcal per kg a day and of fat at roughly 4.5 kcal per kg a day (Wang et al., 2010). That means adding 5 kg of muscle raises resting burn by only about 65 kcal a day, a modest effect that is still worth having.

BMR vs RMR

BMR is measured under strict resting conditions. Resting metabolic rate (RMR) is measured under looser conditions and is the figure most formulas, including these, actually estimate. In everyday use the two terms are often treated as the same.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between BMR and calorie needs?expand_more

BMR is the energy used at complete rest. Your actual daily calorie needs (TDEE) are higher because they include movement, exercise, and digestion.

Which BMR formula is most accurate?expand_more

Mifflin-St Jeor is widely used and performs well in validation studies, but no predictive BMR equation is universally most accurate for every person.

Is the Mifflin-St Jeor formula the most accurate?expand_more

It is a widely used general-population equation, but any formula is an estimate and individual energy needs can differ meaningfully from the prediction.

Can I increase my BMR?expand_more

Increasing or preserving lean mass can affect resting energy expenditure, but the size of the change varies between people; strength training is useful for maintaining muscle and overall fitness, not a guarantee of a specific BMR increase.

How much does BMR typically decline with age?expand_more

BMR tends to decline gradually with age, partly due to natural muscle loss over time — maintaining muscle mass through strength training can help offset this decline.

How do I calculate my BMR by hand?expand_more

Use Mifflin-St Jeor: 10 x weight (kg) + 6.25 x height (cm) - 5 x age, then add 5 for men or subtract 161 for women. An 80 kg, 180 cm, 35-year-old man gets 1,755 kcal a day.

What is the difference between BMR and RMR?expand_more

BMR is measured under strict resting conditions, RMR under less strict ones. Prediction formulas are really estimating resting energy use, so the terms are often used interchangeably.

Why does Harris-Benedict give a different answer?expand_more

It uses different coefficients from a different population. For a 28-year-old woman, 65 kg and 165 cm, Mifflin-St Jeor gives 1,380 kcal and revised Harris-Benedict about 1,439 kcal.

What is a normal BMR?expand_more

It depends on sex, age, height and weight. For a 30-year-old man of 178 cm, Mifflin-St Jeor gives about 1,518 to 1,918 kcal for weights from 55 to 95 kg.

How much does muscle raise BMR?expand_more

Only modestly. Using roughly 13 kcal per kg a day for muscle, 5 kg of extra muscle adds about 65 kcal a day.

529 to Roth IRA Rollover: The Rules and a Worked Timeline

Leftover 529 money no longer has to be stuck. Here are the conditions and how long a full rollover takes.

→ Use the free 529 College Savings Calculator

Short answer: Under SECURE 2.0 you can roll up to $35,000 over your lifetime from a 529 to the beneficiary's Roth IRA if the 529 has been open for at least 15 years. Funds moved must have been in the account at least 5 years, and each year's rollover is capped by the annual Roth IRA contribution limit.

The conditions

  • The 529 account has been open for at least 15 years.
  • The money rolled over (and its earnings) has been in the account at least 5 years.
  • The 529 beneficiary and the Roth IRA owner are the same person.
  • Lifetime limit: $35,000 per beneficiary.
  • Each year's rollover is limited by that year's Roth IRA contribution limit, reduced by any other IRA contributions the beneficiary makes that year. The 2026 IRA limit is $7,500.

Worked timeline

At the 2026 limit of $7,500 a year with no other IRA contributions, a $35,000 rollover takes five years:

YearRolled overRunning total
1$7,500$7,500
2$7,500$15,000
3$7,500$22,500
4$7,500$30,000
5$5,000$35,000

The annual limit is indexed, so it may be higher in later years and shorten the timeline slightly.

Why open a 529 early

The 15-year clock starts when the account opens, so an account opened at a child's birth can qualify while they are still in their teens or twenties. Check whether your state taxes or recaptures benefits on such a rollover.

Frequently asked questions

Can I roll a 529 into a Roth IRA?

Yes, to the beneficiary's own Roth IRA, up to $35,000 over a lifetime, if the 529 has been open at least 15 years.

How long does a $35,000 529-to-Roth rollover take?

At the 2026 limit of $7,500 a year, five years: four years of $7,500 and a final $5,000.

Do all the funds in the 529 qualify for rollover?

No. Funds that have been in the account less than 5 years do not qualify.

Does my state tax a 529 to Roth rollover?

It depends. Some states treat it as a non-qualified withdrawal, so check your state's rules.

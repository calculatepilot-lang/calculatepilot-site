The 20/4/10 Rule for Buying a Car, With Examples

What each part of the 20/4/10 rule means, with worked examples and when to bend it.

→ Use the free Car Affordability Calculator

Short answer: The 20/4/10 rule says put at least 20% down, finance for no more than 4 years and keep total car costs under 10% of income. A $30,000 car under the rule needs roughly $8,700 a month of income.

The three parts

  • 20% down: New cars can lose around 20% of their value in the first year, so a 20% down payment helps avoid owing more than the car is worth.
  • 4 years: A 48-month cap limits total interest and usually clears the loan while the car is still in good shape.
  • 10% of income: Payment, insurance, fuel and maintenance together stay under 10% of income.

Example 1: a $30,000 car

20% down is $6,000, so you finance $24,000. At 6.5% over 48 months the payment is about $569. Add $300 for insurance and running costs and the total is about $869 a month. For that to be 10% of income, you need about $8,692 a month of income.

Example 2: what a budget buys

With $8,000 down and $300 of insurance and running costs, a 48-month loan at 6.5%:

Income basis10% budgetLoan paymentLoan amountMax price
$5,000 take-home$500$200$8,433$16,433
$6,500 gross$650$350$14,759$22,759

Using gross income gives a higher price because gross is larger than take-home. The calculator lets you switch between the two. Both results keep the down payment above 20% of the price.

When to bend the rule

  • If you cannot reach 10%, choose a cheaper car before stretching the term.
  • A 60-month loan is common, but it moves you past the rule and adds interest.
  • A higher interest rate lowers what the same payment buys, so check your APR first.

Frequently asked questions

What is the 20/4/10 rule?

At least 20% down, a loan of no more than 4 years, and total car costs under 10% of income.

Does the 10% include insurance and gas?

Yes. The 10% covers the payment plus insurance, fuel or charging and maintenance.

Is the 20/4/10 rule realistic?

For many buyers it is strict. Treat it as a conservative target and use the calculator to see how far a looser rule changes your budget.

Is the 10% based on gross or take-home pay?

Versions differ. The calculator defaults to take-home and lets you switch to gross.

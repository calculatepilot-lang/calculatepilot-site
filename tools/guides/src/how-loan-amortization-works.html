How Loan Amortization Works: Why Early Payments Are Mostly Interest

The same payment every month, but a different split each time. Here is the mechanism.

→ Use the free Loan Calculator

Short answer: Each payment on an amortizing loan covers that month's interest first, with the rest reducing principal. On a $20,000, 7.5%, 60-month loan the $400.76 payment starts as $125.00 interest and $275.76 principal, and the interest share shrinks every month.

The payment formula

M = P × [ i(1+i)n ] ÷ [ (1+i)n − 1 ], where P is the loan amount, i is the monthly rate (annual rate ÷ 12) and n is the number of months. For $20,000 at 7.5% over 60 months, i = 0.00625 and M = $400.76.

How each payment splits

  1. Interest for the month = balance × i. First month: $20,000 × 0.00625 = $125.00.
  2. Principal = payment − interest = $400.76 − $125.00 = $275.76.
  3. New balance = $20,000 − $275.76 = $19,724.24. Repeat.

Because the balance falls, interest falls and principal rises, even though the payment never changes. Over 60 months the interest totals $4,046.

What this means for you

  • Early extra payments save the most interest. See the table.
  • Paying off a loan early in its life leaves you owing most of the original principal, so selling or refinancing soon after you borrow rarely leaves much equity.
  • Some loans calculate interest daily, which can differ by a few cents from a monthly schedule.

Frequently asked questions

How is a loan payment calculated?

With M = P × [i(1+i)^n] ÷ [(1+i)^n − 1], where i is the monthly rate and n the number of months.

Why is my first loan payment mostly interest?

Interest is charged on the full balance, which is highest at the start. On $20,000 at 7.5%, $125 of the first $400.76 payment is interest.

Does the monthly payment change over time on a fixed-rate loan?

No. The payment stays level, but the split shifts from interest toward principal.

What is an amortization schedule?

A table showing each payment's interest, principal and remaining balance until the loan is paid off.

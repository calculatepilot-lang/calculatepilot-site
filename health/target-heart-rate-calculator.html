How this calculator works

Maximum heart rate is commonly estimated as 220 minus age. Target training zones are then calculated as percentages of that maximum.

Max HR = 220 − age
Moderate intensity: 50-70% of max HR · Vigorous intensity: 70-85% of max HR

Choosing the right training zone

The moderate zone (50-70% of max HR) is typically recommended for building an aerobic base, longer endurance sessions, and active recovery days, since it's sustainable for extended periods without excessive fatigue.

The vigorous zone (70-85%) is associated with greater cardiovascular fitness improvements in shorter timeframes but is harder to sustain for long durations — most training plans mix both zones depending on the day's goal.

A worked example

A 45-year-old: estimated max heart rate is 220 minus 45, or 175 bpm. Moderate zone (50-70%) is roughly 88-123 bpm, and vigorous zone (70-85%) is roughly 123-149 bpm.

Compare to a 25-year-old, with a max HR of 195 bpm: their moderate zone (98-137 bpm) and vigorous zone (137-166 bpm) both run noticeably higher, since target zones scale directly with the age-based max heart rate estimate.

Why heart rate zones matter for training

Training in the moderate zone (50-70% of max heart rate) is generally associated with building aerobic base fitness and is sustainable for longer durations, while the vigorous zone (70-85%) is associated with greater cardiovascular and calorie-burning benefits per minute, but is harder to sustain for extended periods.

Many training programs alternate between zones intentionally — using moderate-intensity sessions for volume and recovery, and vigorous sessions for intensity-focused training, rather than training in just one zone exclusively.

Frequently asked questions

How accurate is the 220-minus-age formula?

It's a widely used population estimate, but actual max heart rate varies by individual — a fitness professional or lab test can provide a more precise number if needed.

What heart rate zone is best for fat loss?

Both moderate and vigorous zones contribute to fat loss primarily through total calories burned — the 'fat-burning zone' concept (lower intensity) is often misunderstood, since higher intensity exercise typically burns more total calories overall even if a smaller percentage comes from fat.

Is the 220-minus-age formula accurate for everyone?

It's a widely used population estimate, but actual max heart rate varies by individual — a supervised fitness test can provide a more personalized number if precision matters for your training.

Which heart rate zone is best for fat loss?

Both zones burn calories effectively; moderate-intensity work burns a higher percentage of calories from fat per minute, while vigorous work burns more total calories per minute — total weekly volume tends to matter more than the specific zone for overall fat loss.

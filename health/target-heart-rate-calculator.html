How this calculator works

  • AHA method (% of max HR): max HR ≈ 220 − age. Moderate intensity is about 50–70% of max; vigorous is about 70–85%.
  • Karvonen method (% of heart rate reserve): reserve = max HR − resting HR; target = resting HR + reserve × intensity. The American College of Sports Medicine classes 40–59% of reserve as moderate and 60–89% as vigorous. Because it uses your resting heart rate, it adjusts for fitness.
  • Max HR alternatives: Tanaka (208 − 0.7 × age) and Gellish (207 − 0.7 × age) fit large datasets better than 220 − age, especially over 40. A measured max from a supervised test is best.

A worked example

A 45-year-old: max HR = 220 − 45 = 175 bpm. Moderate zone (50–70%) is about 88–123 bpm and vigorous (70–85%) about 123–149 bpm. With a resting heart rate of 65, the reserve is 110 bpm, so the Karvonen moderate zone (40–59%) is 109–130 bpm and vigorous (60–89%) is 131–163 bpm.

Choosing the right zone

Moderate-intensity sessions build an aerobic base and are sustainable for longer; vigorous sessions give more fitness benefit per minute but are harder to sustain. Adults are advised to get 150–300 minutes of moderate or 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity a week. Most plans mix both.

Some medicines, such as beta-blockers, lower heart rate and make these zones inaccurate. If you take heart medication or have a heart condition, ask your doctor what heart rate — or perceived effort — to use.

Frequently asked questions

Which is better: % of max or heart rate reserve?expand_more

Heart rate reserve (Karvonen) is more personalized because it accounts for your resting heart rate, which drops as you get fitter. The % of max method is simpler and only needs your age.

How do I check my heart rate during exercise?expand_more

Use a heart-rate monitor or watch, or stop briefly and count your pulse at the wrist or neck for 10 seconds, then multiply by 6. The pulse-check panel shows your 10-second targets.

What is a normal resting heart rate?expand_more

For most adults it's between 60 and 100 beats per minute. Well-trained athletes often have lower resting rates. Check it in the morning before getting out of bed.

How this calculator works

BMI is calculated by dividing weight by the square of height. It's a quick screening tool, not a diagnosis — it doesn't distinguish muscle from fat.

BMI = weight(kg) / height(m)²
Imperial inputs are converted to metric automatically before calculating.

How to interpret your BMI

Under 18.5 is considered underweight, 18.5–24.9 is considered a healthy range, 25–29.9 is considered overweight, and 30 or above is considered obese, according to standard adult BMI categories used by major health organizations worldwide.

BMI doesn't account for muscle mass, bone density, or body composition, so athletes and very muscular people often score higher than their actual body fat would suggest. A bodybuilder at 200 lbs and 5'10" might show a BMI in the 'overweight' range despite having low body fat, simply because muscle is denser than fat.

Age and sex also affect how BMI should be interpreted. Older adults tend to carry more body fat at the same BMI compared to younger adults, and women generally carry a higher percentage of body fat than men at an equivalent BMI. These are population-level patterns worth keeping in mind rather than hard rules.

A worked example

Take someone who is 5'7" (67 inches) and weighs 160 lbs. Using the imperial formula — weight in pounds divided by height in inches squared, multiplied by 703 — gives (160 ÷ 4489) × 703 ≈ 25.1, landing just into the 'overweight' category.

For the same height, the healthy weight range (BMI 18.5–24.9) works out to roughly 118–159 lbs — meaning this person would need to lose about 1-2 lbs to move into the 'healthy weight' range by BMI alone, illustrating how close these category boundaries often sit to someone's actual weight.

In metric terms, a person 170 cm tall (1.7 m) weighing 72 kg would calculate as 72 ÷ (1.7 × 1.7) = 72 ÷ 2.89 ≈ 24.9 — right at the top edge of the healthy range, again showing how a kilogram or two either way can shift the category.

Why BMI has limitations

BMI was originally developed in the 1830s as a population-level statistical tool, not a diagnostic measure for individuals — a distinction that's often lost in how it's used today. It can't distinguish between muscle, fat, bone, and water weight, all of which contribute differently to health.

Two people with an identical BMI can have very different body compositions and health profiles. This is why many clinicians now pair BMI with other measurements — waist circumference, body fat percentage, or metabolic markers — for a fuller picture rather than relying on BMI alone.

What to do with your BMI result

If your BMI falls outside the 'healthy weight' range, treat it as a starting point for a conversation rather than a verdict. A single number doesn't capture activity level, muscle mass, diet quality, or family health history — all of which matter more for actual health outcomes than BMI alone.

For a fuller picture, consider pairing this result with a body fat percentage estimate (see our Body Fat Calculator), tracking waist circumference over time, or discussing your overall health profile with a doctor — especially if BMI, weight trends, or other symptoms are a concern.

If you're using BMI to track progress over time rather than as a one-time snapshot, focus on the trend rather than any single reading — gradual, sustained changes tend to be more meaningful than short-term fluctuations, which can reflect water weight or measurement variation as much as anything else.

Frequently asked questions

Is BMI accurate for everyone?

BMI is a useful screening tool for most adults but can be misleading for athletes, older adults, and people with unusually high or low muscle mass. It doesn't measure body fat directly.

What is a healthy BMI range?

For most adults, a BMI between 18.5 and 24.9 is considered a healthy weight range.

Does BMI differ for men and women?

The standard BMI formula is the same for adult men and women, though body composition differences mean it's interpreted somewhat differently in clinical practice.

Is BMI accurate for children?

No — children and teens require age- and sex-specific BMI percentile charts rather than the adult category thresholds used here, since body composition changes significantly during growth.

Why do athletes often have a 'high' BMI?

Because BMI can't distinguish muscle from fat, and muscle is denser than fat, athletes and highly muscular people frequently score in the 'overweight' or even 'obese' range despite having low body fat percentages.

What should I use instead of or alongside BMI?

Waist circumference, body fat percentage (see our Body Fat Calculator), and waist-to-hip ratio are commonly used alongside BMI for a more complete picture of health risk.

How much does a small weight change affect BMI category?

Often more than people expect — near category boundaries, a difference of just 1-3 lbs (or around 1 kg) can shift someone from one BMI category to the next.

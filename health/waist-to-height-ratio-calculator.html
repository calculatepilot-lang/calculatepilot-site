How this calculator works

WHtR = waist circumference ÷ height, both in the same unit — so the ratio has no units. The result is classified using the NICE adult categories:

  • 0.4 to 0.49: healthy central adiposity — no increased health risks
  • 0.5 to 0.59: increased central adiposity — increased health risks
  • 0.6 or more: high central adiposity — further increased health risks

NICE recommends these classifications for adults with a BMI under 35, of both sexes and all ethnicities, including adults with high muscle mass. Ratios below 0.4 aren't a NICE category; if yours is that low, check your BMI to make sure you're not underweight.

The 'keep your waist below half your height' guideline

Waist-to-height ratio is built around a simple public health message: keeping your waist circumference under half your height is associated with lower cardiometabolic risk. A 2012 meta-analysis by Ashwell and colleagues found WHtR was a better screening tool for cardiometabolic risk than waist circumference or BMI, because it captures fat carried around the middle — which is more strongly linked to health risk than overall body weight.

A worked example

Someone 170 cm tall with a 95 cm waist: 95 ÷ 170 = 0.56, which is "increased central adiposity". Half their height is 85 cm, so a waist below 85 cm would bring the ratio under 0.5 — a reduction of 10 cm.

How it complements other measures

No single measure tells the whole story — waist-to-height ratio, BMI and waist-to-hip ratio each capture a different aspect of body composition and risk. NICE suggests using WHtR alongside BMI. This is a screening tool for general awareness, not a diagnosis; a healthcare provider can interpret it in the context of your full health picture.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to convert units before entering my measurements?expand_more

No — just make sure both waist and height are in the same unit (both inches or both centimetres), since the ratio itself is unitless as long as they match.

Is waist-to-height ratio better than BMI?expand_more

They measure different things. Research suggests waist-to-height ratio is a better indicator of central fat and related cardiometabolic risk, but most health professionals — including NICE — view the two as complementary rather than one replacing the other.

Where exactly should I measure my waist?expand_more

Find the bottom of your lowest rib and the top of your hip bone, and measure around your body midway between them, keeping the tape level and snug but not compressing the skin. Breathe out normally before reading the tape.

What waist-to-height ratio is healthy?expand_more

NICE classes 0.4 to 0.49 as healthy central adiposity for adults. From 0.5 health risks are increased, and from 0.6 they are further increased.

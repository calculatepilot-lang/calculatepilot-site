How this calculator works

The baseline is the National Academies (IOM, 2004) Adequate Intake for total water — water from all drinks and food. About 80% typically comes from drinks and 20% from food, so the headline figure is 80% of the total, plus exercise:

Exercise: extra fluid = minutes ÷ 60 × your sweat rate. Sweat rates vary widely — the American College of Sports Medicine reports roughly 0.3 to 2.4 L an hour depending on intensity, heat and the person — so 0.5 L/hour is only a starting assumption. To measure yours, weigh yourself before and after exercise: the ACSM advises drinking about 1.25–1.5 L for every kg lost (roughly 20–24 oz per lb).

The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) sets lower adult values — 2.5 L for men and 2.0 L for women, plus 300 mL in pregnancy and about 700 mL when breastfeeding — shown for comparison.

A worked example

A 30-year-old man who exercises 45 minutes a day: total water 3.7 L, of which about 3.0 L from drinks. At a sweat rate of 0.5 L/hour, 45 minutes adds 0.375 L, for about 3.3 L (113 oz, 14 cups) from drinks on exercise days.

Signs you may need more water

For most healthy people, drinking when thirsty and with meals meets needs. Pale-yellow urine generally indicates good hydration, while darker yellow suggests you need more. Hot weather, high altitude, fever, vomiting or diarrhea, and pregnancy or breastfeeding all raise fluid needs. People with heart, kidney or liver conditions may be told to limit fluids — follow your doctor's advice.

Frequently asked questions

Does this include water from food and other beverages?expand_more

The total-water figure includes everything; the headline "from drinks" figure is about 80% of it, since food typically supplies around 20%. Drinks include plain water, milk, tea, coffee and other beverages — not only water.

Can I drink too much water?expand_more

Yes, though it's uncommon — drinking very large amounts in a short period can dilute sodium in the blood, a condition called hyponatremia. It's mainly a risk during long endurance events when people drink far more than they sweat.

Is the "8 glasses a day" rule accurate?expand_more

Eight 8-oz glasses (about 1.9 L) is a simple rule of thumb rather than an official recommendation. The National Academies reference for drinks is higher for most adults — about 9 cups for women and 13 for men — though needs vary with activity, climate and diet.

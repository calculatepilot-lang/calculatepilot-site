How this calculator works

The Devine formula, originally developed for medication dosing, is one of the most commonly cited reference formulas for ideal body weight. Each formula starts from a base weight at 5 feet (152.4 cm) and adds a fixed amount per inch above that:

  • Devine (1974): men 50 kg + 2.3 kg per inch over 5 ft · women 45.5 kg + 2.3 kg per inch
  • Robinson (1983): men 52 kg + 1.9 kg per inch · women 49 kg + 1.7 kg per inch
  • Miller (1983): men 56.2 kg + 1.41 kg per inch · women 53.1 kg + 1.36 kg per inch
  • Hamwi (1964): men 48 kg + 2.7 kg per inch · women 45.5 kg + 2.2 kg per inch

The frame option applies the common ±10% convention (originally used with the Hamwi method). This is a population reference, not a personal target — body composition and frame size vary widely.

Limitations of ideal weight formulas

These formulas don't account for muscle mass, bone structure, or individual body composition, so they're best treated as a general reference point rather than a personal goal. They were designed for adults around 5 ft and taller; below that they are less reliable.

A worked example

A man who is 180 cm tall: that's about 70.9 inches, or 10.9 inches over 5 feet. Using the Devine formula: 50 plus (2.3 times 10.9) equals about 75.0 kg, about 165 lb.

For a woman at the same height: 45.5 plus (2.3 times 10.9) equals about 70.5 kg, about 155 lb — illustrating the roughly 10 lb (4.5 kg) difference the formula applies between sexes at the same height.

Ideal weight in clinical use

Clinicians use ideal body weight — usually the Devine formula — for some medication doses and ventilator settings. When someone's actual weight is well above their ideal weight, an adjusted body weight (ideal weight + 0.4 × (actual − ideal)) is sometimes used instead. Medical information, not medical advice: dosing decisions are made by your healthcare team.

Frequently asked questions

Is this formula still considered accurate?expand_more

It's a commonly used clinical reference, particularly for medication dosing, but it's a population average and doesn't account for individual differences in build or muscle mass.

Why do different ideal weight formulas give different results?expand_more

Each formula (Devine, Robinson, Miller, Hamwi) was derived from different population data and uses slightly different constants, so results typically vary by a few kilograms between formulas for the same height. None is definitively 'correct' — treat them as general reference points rather than precise targets.

Is BMI or ideal weight formula more useful?expand_more

Both have similar limitations — neither accounts for muscle mass or individual body composition, so pairing either with other measures like body fat percentage gives a fuller picture.

How does body frame size change ideal weight?expand_more

A common convention subtracts 10% for a small frame and adds 10% for a large frame. You can estimate your frame from your wrist circumference and height with the body frame size calculator.

How this calculator works

TDEE is your BMR multiplied by an activity factor, giving a realistic estimate of the calories you burn on an average day including exercise and daily movement.

TDEE = BMR × activity multiplier. The four BMR equations used (weight W in kg, height H in cm, age A in years, lean body mass LBM in kg):

  • Mifflin-St Jeor (1990): 10W + 6.25H − 5A + 5 for men, − 161 for women. Generally the most accurate of the standard equations for adults without a body-fat measurement.
  • Harris-Benedict, revised (Roza & Shizgal, 1984): men 88.362 + 13.397W + 4.799H − 5.677A; women 447.593 + 9.247W + 3.098H − 4.330A.
  • Katch-McArdle: 370 + 21.6 × LBM. Uses lean mass, so it needs a body-fat percentage.
  • Cunningham (1980): 500 + 22 × LBM. Also lean-mass based; often used for very lean, muscular people.

The thermic effect of food (the energy used to digest what you eat) is shown as about 10% of TDEE; the rest above BMR is physical activity, including everyday movement.

Adjusting TDEE for your specific goal

For weight loss, a common approach is eating 15-20% below TDEE, creating a moderate, sustainable deficit rather than an extreme one that's harder to maintain and more likely to cause muscle loss alongside fat loss.

For muscle gain, eating 10-15% above TDEE alongside resistance training is a common approach, aiming to minimize excess fat gain while still providing enough surplus energy to support muscle growth. The weekly weight change shown uses a rough 7,700 kcal per kg of body weight; real results vary, and weight loss usually slows as your TDEE falls.

A worked example

A 40-year-old woman, 70 kg, 165 cm, moderately active: Mifflin-St Jeor BMR is 10 × 70 + 6.25 × 165 − 5 × 40 − 161 = about 1,370 calories, and TDEE at the 1.55 activity multiplier works out to about 2,124 calories/day.

Using a 30/30/40 macro split on that TDEE gives targets of roughly 159 g protein, 71 g fat, and 212 g carbohydrates per day. For a personalized breakdown, try our Macro Calculator.

Practical considerations for your goal

Once you know your TDEE, adjusting from it is straightforward: eating at TDEE maintains weight, eating below it (typically a 15-25% deficit) supports fat loss, and eating above it (typically a 10-20% surplus) supports muscle or weight gain.

Very large deficits or surpluses tend to be less sustainable and can work against the goal — extreme deficits risk muscle loss and metabolic slowdown, while excessive surpluses lead to more fat gain than muscle gain. Very-low-calorie diets (about 800 calories a day or less) should only be followed under medical supervision. Medical information, not medical advice: these are estimates for general guidance.

Frequently asked questions

How is TDEE different from BMR?expand_more

BMR is calories burned at complete rest. TDEE adds in activity, exercise, and digestion to estimate your true total daily calorie burn.

Which BMR formula should I use?expand_more

Mifflin-St Jeor is the usual default for adults. If you know your body-fat percentage — especially if you're very lean or muscular — Katch-McArdle or Cunningham can be more accurate because they are based on lean body mass.

Is the macro split shown here right for everyone?expand_more

It's a reasonable general-purpose starting point, but optimal macro splits vary by goal (muscle gain, endurance, fat loss) and individual preference.

How often should I recalculate my TDEE?expand_more

Recalculate whenever your weight changes significantly (every 10-15 lbs) or if your activity level changes meaningfully, since TDEE shifts as your body composition and lifestyle change.

Why is my actual weight trend different from what my TDEE predicts?expand_more

Formula-based TDEE is an estimate — actual metabolism varies by individual, so it's normal to need to adjust your calorie target up or down after a few weeks of real-world tracking.

How much of a calorie deficit should I use for fat loss?expand_more

A moderate deficit of roughly 15-25% below TDEE is generally more sustainable and better preserves muscle mass than a very aggressive deficit.

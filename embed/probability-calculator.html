    Kolmogorov’s three axioms

    All of modern probability rests on three rules, formalised by Andrey Kolmogorov in 1933. This calculator rejects any input that breaks them.

    1. Non-negativity

    P(E) ≥ 0

    The probability of any event is never negative.

    2. Unit measure

    P(Ω) = 1

    Something in the sample space must happen, so the total probability is exactly 1.

    3. Additivity

    P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) if A ∩ B = ∅

    For events that cannot occur together, probabilities add.

    Mutually exclusive vs independent events

    Two of the most confused ideas in probability. They are close to opposites.

    PropertyMutually exclusiveIndependent
    MeaningA and B cannot both happen: A ∩ B = ∅Knowing A happened tells you nothing about B
    Intersection P(A ∩ B)0P(A) × P(B)
    Union P(A ∪ B)P(A) + P(B)P(A) + P(B) − P(A)P(B)
    Conditional P(A | B)0P(A)
    Can both apply?Only if P(A) = 0 or P(B) = 0. If both events are possible, being mutually exclusive means knowing one happened tells you the other did not — so they are dependent.

    Worked examples

    Each is checked by hand and can be reproduced above.

    Rare-condition screening

    A condition affects 1 in 1,000 people. A test detects it 95% of the time and gives a false positive 5% of the time. Someone tests positive — what is the chance they have it?

    P(B) = 0.95×0.001 + 0.05×0.999 = 0.05090
    P(A|B) = 0.00095 ÷ 0.05090
    = 0.0187 (about 1.87%)

    Most positives are false positives because the condition is so rare. Use the Bayes’ theorem tab.

    At least one six in four rolls

    Rolling a fair die four times: the chance of at least one six.

    P(no six in one roll) = 5/6
    P(no six in four) = (5/6)⁴ = 0.4823
    P(at least one six) = 1 − 0.4823 = 0.5177

    Use the Odds tab with p = 1/6 (0.1667) and 4 trials.

    Two independent backups

    Each of two independent backup systems fails with probability 0.02.

    Both fail: 0.02 × 0.02 = 0.0004
    At least one fails: 0.02 + 0.02 − 0.0004 = 0.0396

    Enter 0.02 and 0.02 on the Two events tab, independent.

    What is probability?

    Probability measures how likely an event is, on a scale from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). It can be written as a decimal, a percentage, a fraction, odds, or a frequency such as “1 in 20”.

    The core rules

    • Complement: P(not A) = 1 − P(A).
    • Addition (either event): P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B). The intersection is subtracted so it is not counted twice.
    • Multiplication (both events), independent: P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B).
    • Conditional probability: P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) ÷ P(B), for P(B) > 0.
    • Bayes’ theorem: P(A | B) = P(B | A) × P(A) ÷ P(B), where P(B) = P(B | A)P(A) + P(B | not A)P(not A).
    • Odds: odds in favour = p ÷ (1 − p); p = a ÷ (a + b) for odds a : b.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is the difference between mutually exclusive and independent events?expand_more

    Mutually exclusive events cannot happen together, so P(A ∩ B) = 0. Independent events can happen together, and one occurring does not change the probability of the other, so P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B). If both have non-zero probability, events cannot be both.

    Why can’t I just add probabilities for “A or B”?expand_more

    If the events can overlap, adding counts the overlap twice. The addition rule subtracts P(A ∩ B) once to correct this. Adding alone is only right for mutually exclusive events.

    What does Bayes’ theorem do?expand_more

    It updates a prior probability when you observe new evidence. It reverses a conditional probability: from P(B | A), how likely the evidence is if A is true, to P(A | B), how likely A is given the evidence, taking into account how common A was to begin with.

    What is the gambler’s fallacy?expand_more

    It is the mistaken belief that past independent outcomes change future ones — for example that heads is “due” after a run of tails. For independent events, P(A | B) = P(A): the coin has no memory.

    How do I convert probability to odds?expand_more

    Odds in favour are p ÷ (1 − p). A probability of 0.4 gives odds of 0.4 ÷ 0.6 = 2 to 3 in favour, or 3 to 2 against. Use the Odds tab to convert in either direction.

    Can a probability be greater than 1 or negative?expand_more

    No. Every probability lies between 0 and 1 inclusive (0% to 100%). The calculator rejects values outside that range, and joint probabilities that are impossible given P(A) and P(B).

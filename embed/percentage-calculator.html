    The three ideas behind every percentage problem

    percent

    1. Percent means “per hundred”

    P% is the fraction P ÷ 100. To find a portion, scale the base by that fraction.

    r = 18.5 ÷ 100 = 0.185
    Y = 0.185 × 450 = 83.25
    Check: 83.25 ÷ 450 × 100 = 18.5% ✓
    swap_vert

    2. Increases and decreases are not symmetrical

    Percentage change is measured against the starting value, so undoing a change needs a different percentage.

    100 → +50% → 150
    150 → −50% → 75 (not 100)
    To return: 150 → 100 is −33.33%
    history

    3. Reverse percentages divide, not subtract

    To recover the original before a change, divide the final value by the growth factor.

    Final = X × (1 ± r)
    X = Final ÷ (1 ± r)
    $120 after 20% off: 120 ÷ 0.80 = $150

    Worked examples

    Illustrative figures, each checked by hand.

    storefront

    Markup vs margin

    A retailer adds a 30% markup to a $100 cost, giving a $130 price.

    Markup = 30 ÷ 100 = 30% of cost
    Margin = 30 ÷ 130 = 23.08% of price

    The same $30 profit is a different percentage depending on the base.

    trending_down

    Real pay after inflation

    A wage rises 3.0% while prices rise 4.5%.

    Real change = 1.030 ÷ 1.045 − 1
    = −1.44% (about)

    Dividing growth factors is more accurate than subtracting 4.5 from 3.0.

    sell

    Discount, then tax

    An $80 item is 25% off and then 8% sales tax is added.

    80 × 0.75 = 60.00
    60.00 × 1.08 = $64.80

    Tax is calculated on the discounted price, so the order matters only if the store applies them differently.

    What is a percentage?

    A percentage is a number expressed as a fraction of 100. “18.5%” means 18.5 out of every 100, or 0.185. Percentages make it easy to compare parts of wholes of different sizes.

    The four formulas

    • P% of X: Y = (P ÷ 100) × X
    • X is what % of Y: % = (X ÷ Y) × 100
    • Percentage change: % change = (new − old) ÷ |old| × 100
    • Reverse percentage: original = final ÷ (1 ± rate ÷ 100), using + for an increase and − for a decrease

    Frequently asked questions

    Why aren’t percentage increases and decreases symmetrical?expand_more

    Percentage change is measured against the starting value. Going from 100 to 125 is +25%; going from 125 back to 100 is −25 ÷ 125 = −20%, because the base is now 125. A 25% increase therefore needs a 20% decrease to return to the original.

    What is the difference between percentage points and percent change?expand_more

    Percentage points are the simple difference between two percentages: going from 4% to 5% is 1 percentage point. Percent change compares them proportionally: 5 is 25% more than 4. Mixing the two up is a common source of misleading headlines.

    How do I calculate a discount before sales tax?expand_more

    Multiply the price by (1 − discount rate) to get the sale price, then multiply by (1 + tax rate). For $80 with 25% off and 8% tax: 80 × 0.75 × 1.08 = $64.80. Some jurisdictions tax the pre-discount price in certain cases, so check the local rules.

    How do I find the original price before a percentage change?expand_more

    Divide the final value by the growth factor. After a 20% discount the factor is 0.80, so a $120 price came from 120 ÷ 0.80 = $150. Subtracting 20% of $120 would give the wrong answer.

    Can a percentage be greater than 100%?expand_more

    Yes. 150% of a number is one and a half times it, and a value that doubles has increased by 100%. Only percentages of a fixed whole (such as a share of a total) are limited to 100%.

    What if the starting value is zero?expand_more

    Percentage change from zero is undefined because it divides by zero, and “X is what percent of 0” is undefined for the same reason. Report the absolute change instead.

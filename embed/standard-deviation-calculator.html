    Population vs sample standard deviation

    The two formulas differ only in the divisor. The sample version divides by n − 1 so that the variance estimate is not biased low when the mean is itself estimated from the same data.

    Population SD (σ) divisor N

    Use when your list is every member of the group you care about — every employee, every unit in a batch.

    σ = √[ Σ(xᵢ − μ)² ÷ N ]

    Sample SD (s) Bessel-corrected, divisor n − 1

    Use when your list is a sample drawn from a larger population — the usual case in surveys and experiments.

    s = √[ Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² ÷ (n − 1) ]

    Why n − 1? Deviations are measured from the sample mean, which sits closer to the data than the true population mean does, so Σ(x − x̄)² tends to be smaller than Σ(x − μ)². Dividing by n − 1 instead of n corrects that on average: the sample variance s² is then an unbiased estimator of σ². (The square root s is still very slightly biased for small n.)

    Worked example: checking a batch of ball bearings

    Eight bearings are measured (mm): 25.002, 25.008, 24.996, 25.004, 25.000, 24.998, 25.006, 25.002. How consistent is the process? (Load this data from the examples menu to see it above.)

    1. 1

      Mean

      Add the eight values and divide by 8.

      x̄ = 200.016 ÷ 8 = 25.002 mm
    2. 2

      Squared deviations

      Deviations are 0, +0.006, −0.006, +0.002, −0.002, −0.004, +0.004, 0.

      Σ(x − x̄)² = 0.000112
    3. 3

      Sample variance and SD

      The bearings are a sample from a production run, so divide by n − 1 = 7.

      s² = 0.000112 ÷ 7 = 0.000016 → s = 0.004 mm

    Describing variability

    What is standard deviation?

    Standard deviation measures how far values typically sit from their mean. A small standard deviation means the values cluster tightly around the average; a large one means they are spread out. It is expressed in the same units as the data, which is why it is used more often than variance.

    How to calculate standard deviation

    • Find the mean of the values.
    • Subtract the mean from each value and square the result.
    • Add the squares to get the sum of squares (SS).
    • Divide SS by n − 1 (sample) or N (population) to get the variance.
    • Take the square root of the variance.

    The 95% confidence interval for the mean

    For a sample, the interval is x̄ ± t × s ÷ √n, where t is the Student t critical value with n − 1 degrees of freedom (2.262 for n = 10). It is shown only for the sample setting, because a population mean is known exactly and needs no interval.

    Frequently asked questions

    Why do we divide by n − 1 for a sample?expand_more

    Because the deviations are measured from the sample mean rather than the unknown true mean, the raw sum of squares runs low. Dividing by n − 1 (Bessel's correction) makes the sample variance an unbiased estimate of the population variance.

    What is the difference between standard deviation and standard error?expand_more

    Standard deviation describes the spread of individual values. Standard error of the mean, s ÷ √n, describes how much the sample mean itself would vary from sample to sample, so it shrinks as the sample grows while the standard deviation does not.

    How much do outliers affect standard deviation?expand_more

    A great deal, because deviations are squared. One extreme value can inflate the standard deviation substantially. If outliers are a concern, compare it with the interquartile range, which is far less sensitive.

    Can standard deviation be negative?expand_more

    No. It is the square root of a sum of squares, so it is zero or positive. It is zero only when every value is identical.

    Should I choose sample or population?expand_more

    Choose population only if your list contains every member of the group you are describing. If it is a sample used to learn about a larger group, choose sample. When in doubt, sample is the standard choice.

    What does the coefficient of variation tell me?expand_more

    It expresses the standard deviation as a percentage of the mean, so you can compare the relative spread of data measured on different scales. It is only meaningful when the mean is not close to zero and the data is on a ratio scale.

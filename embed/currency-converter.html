The Hidden FX Spread: Why "Zero Fee" Transfers Can Be Misleading

Many banks and exchange desks advertise "no commission" or "$0 transfer fees" but set their exchange rate below the mid-market rate. That markup is a cost even though it never appears as a line item — it simply reduces what your recipient receives.

How to check: compare the rate you're offered with the mid-market rate at the same moment. The percentage gap is the markup — enter it above.

Large Transfers: When Rate Movements Matter

On large one-off transfers — a property purchase, tuition, or moving savings abroad — even small daily moves in the rate can change the cost by hundreds or thousands. Some providers offer forward contracts to lock a rate for a future payment date, and limit orders to convert only when a target rate is reached.

Reference rates: central banks such as the European Central Bank publish daily reference rates, useful as a neutral benchmark.

Bank rates vs. market rates

The exchange rate you see quoted by financial news sources is typically the 'mid-market' rate — the midpoint between buy and sell prices on the wholesale currency market. Banks and currency exchange services usually apply a markup on top of this rate, plus sometimes an explicit fee, meaning the rate you actually receive when exchanging money is often less favorable than the quoted market rate.

For significant currency exchanges, it's worth comparing rates across a few providers, since the markup applied can vary meaningfully between banks, exchange kiosks, and online services.

Frequently asked questions

Why doesn't this calculator show live exchange rates automatically?expand_more

Exchange rates fluctuate constantly, and displaying a rate that isn't truly live could be misleading — entering the current rate yourself from a trusted source ensures you're always working with an up-to-date figure.

Why is the rate I get from my bank different from the rate I see online?expand_more

Banks and exchange services typically apply a markup above the 'mid-market' rate quoted by financial data sources, plus sometimes an additional fee — this is standard practice and part of how currency exchange services generate revenue.

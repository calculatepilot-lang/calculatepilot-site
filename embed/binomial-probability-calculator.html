    Governing equations and conditions

    The equations

    Probability mass functionP(X = k) = C(n, k) · pᵏ · (1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ
    Cumulative distributionP(X ≤ k) = Σᵢ₌₀ᵏ C(n, i) · pⁱ · qⁿ⁻ⁱ
    Mean and varianceμ = np, σ² = npq

    Four conditions for a binomial model

    • Fixed number of trials. n is set in advance.
    • Two outcomes. Each trial is a success or a failure.
    • Constant probability. p is the same on every trial.
    • Independence. One trial’s outcome does not affect another’s.

    Lot inspection

    A batch has a 2% defect rate. An inspector tests 50 units and rejects the batch if more than 2 are defective.

    P(X ≤ 2) with n = 50, p = 0.02
    = 0.36417 + 0.37160 + 0.18580 = 0.92157
    P(reject) = P(X > 2) = 0.0784

    Clinical response

    A treatment works for 70% of patients. Among 30 patients, what is the chance that at least 25 respond?

    n = 30, p = 0.70, k = 25
    P(X ≥ 25) = 0.0766 (about 7.7%)
    Mean = 21 responders

    Packet loss

    Each of 64 packets is lost independently with probability 0.05. What is the chance more than 5 are lost?

    n = 64, p = 0.05, k = 5
    P(X ≤ 5) = 0.89990
    P(X > 5) = 0.1001 (about 10.0%)

    What is the binomial distribution?

    The binomial distribution gives the probability of getting exactly k successes in n independent trials when each trial succeeds with the same probability p. Typical examples are the number of heads in coin tosses, defective items in a sample, or patients who respond to a treatment.

    How to calculate a binomial probability

    • Work out the binomial coefficient C(n, k) = n! ÷ (k!(n − k)!), the number of ways to choose which trials succeed.
    • Multiply by pᵏ, the probability that those k trials all succeed.
    • Multiply by (1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ, the probability that the other trials all fail.
    • For a cumulative probability, add the terms over the range of k values you need.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is the difference between the binomial and hypergeometric distributions?expand_more

    The binomial model assumes independent trials with a constant probability, as with sampling with replacement or from a very large population. The hypergeometric distribution models sampling without replacement from a finite population, where each draw changes the odds of the next. When the sample is a small fraction of the population (commonly under 5%), the binomial is a good approximation.

    When can I use a normal approximation?expand_more

    A common rule of thumb is that both np and n(1 − p) should be at least 5 (some texts use 10). Then X is approximately normal with mean np and standard deviation √(npq), usually with a continuity correction of ±0.5. This calculator gives the exact answer, so no approximation is needed.

    How does the calculator avoid overflow for large n?expand_more

    Instead of multiplying huge factorials, it works with the logarithm of the binomial coefficient (using the log-gamma function) and exponentiates at the end. The exact coefficient shown in the steps is computed separately with integer arithmetic.

    What happens when p is 0 or 1?expand_more

    The outcome is certain: if p = 0 every trial fails, so P(X = 0) = 1; if p = 1 every trial succeeds, so P(X = n) = 1. The variance is zero and the distribution is a single point.

    How do I find the probability of at least one success?expand_more

    Use the complement: P(X ≥ 1) = 1 − P(X = 0) = 1 − (1 − p)ⁿ. Set k = 1 and choose P(X ≥ k) to see it directly.

    What if my trials are not independent?expand_more

    Then the binomial model does not apply exactly. Depending on the situation, a hypergeometric or other model may be more suitable. Independence and a constant probability are essential assumptions.
